## ABSTRACT

Reducing greenhouse gas (GHG) emissions from cropland while ensuring food security is a critical component of the sustainable development goal. Previous studies demonstrated the huge potential of cropland related GHG mitigation and yield production by adopting a broad range of technologies, integrating effective management practices and switching crop production modes. However, the mechanisms by which labor aging affects lowcarbon crop yield remain unclear in labor-intensive agricultural sector of China, especially under the rapid demographic aging globally. This study aims to fill the gap by employing the Tobit regression and proposing structural equation model (SEM), taking site investigation samples in rural China (smallholders with rice, wheat and maize cropping, n = 2889) as cases, given the serious labor aging occurred in China's rural area and its crop yield dominated by smallholders. The method can be transferable to any other countries facing the challenge of rural labor aging and GHG emissions mitigation. The results showed that the impact of agricultural labor aging on low-carbon agricultural yield was significantly mediated by agricultural machinery. Increased agricultural mechanization contributed to higher emissions while improving crop yields, but its adoption was negatively affected by aging. Aging, especially for the physical limitations and a reliance on empirical practices and collectivist knowledge communication of smallholders in this study, led to a reduction in the adoption of agricultural machinery, which indirectly decreased cropland GHG emissions through decreased fossil energy consumption, yet hindered crop yield. Finally, the policy implications, including enhancing agricultural machinery training, green energy machinery, and subsidies, were raised. This study highlights the climate change mitigation opportunities should consider the important role of population aging especially in rural areas.

## 1. Introduction

The agriculture sector plays a critical role in meeting food security and greenhouse gas (GHG) mitigation, in line with Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs) 12 and 13, due to the fact that one-third of global GHG emissions come from agri-food systems [1-3]. Huge potential for climate mitigation of cropland and increasing food output by adopting a broad range of technologies [4], integrating effective management practices [5–8], and switching crop production modes have been identified [9,10]. In China, the“dual carbon"goal proposed to achieve“peak carbon"emission by 2030 and“carbon neutral"by 2060. Thus, a number of policy measures have been proposed, such as the“Methane Emission Control Action Plan" [11] and the“2025 Fertilizer Reduction Action Plan" [12]. Internationally, the Paris Agreement aims to limit global warming well below 2 °C, preferably to 1.5 °C. Reducing agricultural GHG emissions is one of the key approaches to achieve these goals.

Agriculture in China is typically labor-intensive, and population aging may hinder efforts to optimize production and reduce emissions. This is partly because older farmers often have lower levels of education, which can limit their ability to adopt modern agricultural practices and technologies [13–16]. Japan, with the world's highest aging rate (nearly 30 % of its population aged 65 and above), has proposed a series of policies to address human resources in agriculture and cropland emissions [17]. This challenge is still serious in China due to its agriculture is dominated by smallholders with <10 hectares of cropland, and the rural population over 65 years old has already reached 17.7 % in 2020 [15, 18–20]. Besides, China's agriculture contributes 11 % of global total emissions [21,22], in which three major food crops (rice, wheat and maize), occupying 90 % of the national grain production [23], contributed 670 million tons of CO2 eq in 2018 according to Xia et al. [24]. To this content, understanding the impact mechanism of rural labor aging on low-carbon crop yield in China can bring more insightful implications towards meeting SDGs, also globally.

Although studies have been conducted to examine the relationship between demographic trends and future carbon emissions in China, this challenge in countries other than China has also been explored [25–26]. For example, a study in Spain found that the adoption of new machinery proposed by the Common Agricultural Policy does not directly influence the reduction of emissions [27]. A study in the United States found that GHG emissions per capita increased with age until the 60 s, and then emissions tend to decrease [28]. Studies focusing on the relationship among rural population aging, cropland GHG emissions, and crop yield have drawn inconsistent conclusions. For instance, several studies concluded that the aging of the agricultural labor force significantly contributed to GHG emissions due to older farmers tend to adjust land use patterns and cultivation structure, and increase fertilizer applications [29–31]. While some studies showed that rural aging had a significant emission reduction effect due to older farmers are more skilled in farming, which results in less unnecessary energy inputs in agriculture and low agricultural carbon emissions [32-33]. With regard to the impact of aging on crop yield, previous study concluded that rural aging could increase agricultural labor productivity due to rural aging promoted the modernization of agricultural production, and ultimately increasing agricultural labor productivity [34]. However, some studies showed that rural aging can decrease the agricultural labor productivity. For instance, with 1 % increase in the rural aging ratio in northwest China, grain yield decreased by 9.4 kg due to smallholders' migration to cities for higher incomes [35]. A recent national scale study in China showed that 1 % increase of rural aging ratio decreased agricultural output and productivity by 5 % and 4 %, respectively, which can be attributed to that aging led to a 4 % reduction in farm size through the transfer of land ownership and land abandonment [15]. Among the above studies, the STIRPAT model and threshold expansion model were commonly employed. These studies generally neglected emissions caused by nitrogen deposition, including the volatilization, leaching and runoff losses of nitrogen fertilizer, and lacked the adequate consideration on the variability of individual farm. Besides, the above studies mainly adopted the statistical data estimated through partial sampling, which disregards the variability among individual farms related to agricultural practices [36]. While survey-based data can provide more detailed characteristics related to study samples, which have been adopted to analyze the relationship between rural aging and sustainability [15], rural aging, urbanization and food security [35], etc. However, an in-depth survey data-based study on the complexity of the driving effects between rural labor aging and low-carbon crop yield is still lacking.

Under such a circumstance, we first selected and purified data from China Rural Household Tracking Survey (CRHPS) survey samples. The cropland GHG emissions for three major crops from both direct and indirect pathways, as well as their average crop yield per hectare were calculated. Then, the Tobit regression model and structural equation model (SEM) were used to quantify the link between rural labor aging, crop yield, and cropland GHG emissions. The methodology we constructed can be used to evaluate the impacts of rural labor aging in big agricultural economies facing the similar issues in the world, such as India, Brazil, Russia and Italy. Finally, policy implications and limitations were discussed. The rest of the article is organized as follows. Section 2 describes data sources, variable selection, and the methods used for the analysis. Section 3 shows the results and discussion. Section 4 includes the policy implications and the limitations. Section 5 summarizes the main conclusions.

## 2. Variables and methods

## 2.1. Data sources

The data used in this paper were the latest publicly available data from the 2017 China Rural Household Tracking Survey (CRHPS), which is from Chinese Family Database (CFD) of Zhejiang University, and China Household Finance Survey (CHFS) conducted by the Survey and Research Center for China Household Finance at the Southwestern University of Finance and Economics (SWUFE). The available database enforcing the requirements of this study include China Family Panel (CFPS), China Labor-force Dynamics Survey (CLDS), and China Rural Revitalization (CRRS). However, CFPS and CLDS survey data do not include fundamental agricultural indicators. And the CRRS does not match the data needed for this study, and lacks key indicators such as fertilizer application rates and crop yields. CRHPS contains detailed information on rural households, such as the characteristics of each household head, each household's cropland area and production of three major crops (rice, wheat and maize), the amount of fertilizer used annually, agricultural subsidies, total household income, etc. Therefore, we selected the most recent available CRHPS data to conduct this study. This database is openly available at http://ssec.zju.edu.cn/datas et/CRHPS/. Carbon emissions were calculated using the emission factors from previous studies listed in Supplementary Information (SI).

## 2.2. Variable selection and description

The samples were selected and purified according to the following steps. Firstly, only rural households cultivating the three main crops (rice, wheat and maize) were retained. Secondly, variables that included the characteristics of household head, household and farming management were selected. Finally, samples with missing values and outliers were excluded, resulting in a total of 2889 valid samples from 29 provinces, excluding Tibet, Xinjiang, Hong Kong, Macao and Taiwan. The descriptions of the final selected variables were as follows.

Fertilizer is a crucial input for farmers to improve productivity [37], especially in China [38], which is also a major source of cropland GHG emissions [39]. Therefore, we chose fertilizer as the main source of cropland GHG emissions, which can be divided into nitrogen (N), phosphorus (P), and potassium (K) fertilizers. Additionally, the GHG emissions from three major crops during production, transportation, and cultivation and N2O emissions from the soil were also considered. Then, the total calculated cropland GHG emissions serve as the dependent variable. In addition, we also calculated the sum of the crop yield of the three main crops as one of the dependent variables. Despite the variability of cropland GHG emissions across years, Liang et al. showed that the cropland GHG emissions remained relatively stable from 2006 to 2016 [40], indicating that single-year data used in this study can be representative of the analysis of rural labor aging and cropland GHG emissions.

We chose the age of the householder as a reference when considering the independent variable, since smallholders constitute over 98 % of the main agricultural management in China, with the householder being the main decision-maker in agricultural production. In rural China, the proportion of people older than 65 tripled from 1990 to 2020, reaching about 18 % by 2020 [15]. And according to WHO, the region is considered an aging society if the proportion of people over 60 years reaches 10 % or the proportion of people over 65 years reaches 7 %. Therefore, this study follows Ren et al. [15] to take the 65 years as a cutoff. Thus, we employed a dummy variable following Wang and Tian [41], taking 65 years old as the division standard [15]. And we categorized smallholders into old and young laborers, with 1 for smallholders older than or equal to 65 and 0 for otherwise.

Here, household head characteristics, household characteristics, farming management characteristics, and province dummy variables were selected as control variables. We employed gender, education level, and physical health condition to indicate characteristics of the household head included [30,42]. While the household characteristics variables contained household size and the average annual income per household member [43,44]. Finally, the farming management characteristic variables included agricultural subsidies, the percentage of household members working in agriculture, the percentage of farming time per year [44,45], and agricultural machinery, which can reduce fertilizer consumption and enhance productivity [44]. We defined mechanized fertilization as “agricultural machinery"referring to Xiao and Luo [44]. According to the definition in CRHPS database, respondents who selected "ploughing or fertilizing" or “"sowing" were classified as using mechanized fertilization. In contrast, those who selected other options were classified as not using mechanized fertilization. To avoid the errors caused by the differences in units of data, we took the logarithm of average income and subsidies variables. The definition and descriptive statistics for all variables were shown in Table 1.

## 2.3. Cropland GHG emissions calculation

We calculated cropland GHG emissions produced during the cultivation and harvest (rice, wheat and maize), including both upstream emissions from fertilizer production and transportation and field emissions. The system boundary was shown in Fig. 1. Firstly, we calculated the upstream emissions of N, P and K fertilizers using the ratios in Liu et al. [46] and emission factor in Chen et al. [47], respectively. Secondly, we employed emission factors from Lu et al. [48] to account for CH4 emissions from paddy land, in which rice varieties and regional differences were considered. Thirdly, we calculated the indirect and direct emissions of N2O during cultivation of rice, wheat and maize referring to the empirical formula in Chen et al. [47] and the conversion factors in Menegat et al. [49]. It is important to note that while the emission factor approach provides a standardized and practical methodology for large-scale assessments, it inherently carries limitations regarding region-specific variations. Thus, we have integrated region- and crop-specific data to improve accuracy. Finally, CO2, CH4 and N2O emissions were converted to CO2 eq based on the latest conversion factors in the 6th IPCC, in which GWP of CO2, CH4 and N2O are 1, 27.9 and 273, respectively. More detailed calculations were included in SI.

## 3. Results and discussions

## 3.1. Features of China's rural aging and low-carbon crop yield

Previous studies found that aging exacerbated agricultural carbon emissions and reduced crop yield [33,52]. Besides, according to these studies, the peak of agricultural GHG emissions in China occurred around 2016 [33], which is close to the year of this study. Features of China's rural aging, cropland GHG emissions and crop yields in 2017 are calculated and shown in Fig. 2.

The China's rural aging condition was shown in Fig. 2(a). It should be noted that the aging rate is only for the part of the survey and does not represent the level of a province. The averaging aging rate was 46.0 %, and the rural population aging in Jiangsu, Shanghai, Zhejiang and Anhui in the Yangtze River Delta region were most considerable, with an aging rate larger than 56.0 %. With regard to cropland GHG emissions, shown in Fig. 2(b), Heilongjiang, Jilin and Ningxia had the highest average cropland GHG emissions, Shanghai had the lowest, and the rest of the provinces had average cropland GHG emissions around 2t CO2 eq. Fig. 2 (c) shows that the average total yield of the three major crops cultivated by smallholders was approximately 8970 kg/ha. Jilin had the highest yield, followed by Zhejiang, and the lowest was in Qinghai.

## 3.2. Direct impact of aging on low-carbon crop yield

A total of 2889 samples were finally selected in this study. To assess potential multicollinearity issues among the independent variables that could bias regression estimates, their variance inflation factors (VIF) were measured (Table S3 and S4). The maximum and mean values of VIF calculated were 1.18 and 1.09, respectively, satisfying the evaluation criterion of the absence of severe multicollinearity (VIF < 5), indicating the absence of severe multicollinearity concerns and allowing for subsequent baseline regression. And then, the relationship among aging and cropland GHG emissions, and crop yield, were analyzed (Table 2).

The Tobit regression demonstrated a negative correlation between aging and cropland GHG emissions in first column of Table 2. After adding the controlling for province fixed effects, the results also demonstrated the negative correlation between aging and cropland GHG emissions in second column of Table 2. It showed that the aging significantly reduced cropland GHG emissions by 0.158 t CO2 eq (p < 0.1). The same conclusions were reached in Germany and the United States [53,54]. For instance, a study in Germany found that aging accelerated the conversion of agricultural land to forests, which can achieve the carbon sequestration rate of 1.65 Mg C ha⁻¹ yr-¹ [53]. These results can be explained by the fact that the older smallholders were more skilled in farming, and had a higher risk perception that excessive fertilizer application is harmful to human health [32,55]. Although aging may reduce physical capacity, these experience-driven efficiencies dominated GHG mitigation outcomes.

Another reason for cropland GHG emissions reduction by aging can be attributed to that the older smallholders were generally frugal and conservative in consumption, which tend to prioritize cost-effectiveness, especially in rural areas. They prefer low-carbon modes, thus leading to conservative inputs such as fertilizers [56]. Most older smallholders farm for subsistence, and their cropland GHG emissions were smaller than those of intensive large-scale commercial farms [57].

Finally, older smallholders increasingly rely on new types of agribusinesses, such as specialized farmers' cooperatives, specialized households and leading agricultural enterprises. These agribusinesses have made agricultural products cheaper and better quality, leading older smallholders to reduce the inputs to some extent [58]. It can be concluded that older smallholders may tend to transfer their cropland to new types of agribusinesses as a cost-effective strategy to reduce their own inputs, especially given the high production costs. Ofolsha et al. [59] found that while cooperative participation was not associated with age, it indirectly accelerated the transition by lowering GHG emissions through optimized inputs to reduce physical or financial burdens.

Besides, we found that income and subsidies were both significantly positive with cropland GHG emissions (p < 0.01), with coefficients equal to 0.146 and 0.756, respectively. This indicates that increase in household income contributed to the increase in GHG emissions intensity. The coefficients of farming time and machinery application were also positively significant (p < 0.01) at 2.329 and 0.485, respectively, indicating that they played an important role in cropland GHG emissions reduction. Interestingly, the coefficient of gender was significant (p < 0.01) at —0.561, indicating that female smallholders could effectively reduce cropland GHG emissions, which was consistent with Mensah et al. [60]. This may be due to women's greater adaptive capacity and efficient resource utilization. In Tanzania and Uganda, female smallholders had demonstrated remarkable resilience under climate change and had also taken proactive measures to reduce agricultural emissions [61]. According to the International Union for Conservation of Nature, if women smallholders have access to more agricultural training and finance, crop yields would increase by 20 to 30 percent, and GHG emissions could be reduced by 2.1 gigatons by 2050 [62].

We found that aging significantly increased crop yield by 580 kg/ha (converted unit) in the fourth column of Table 2 after adding the province fixed effects (p < 0.05). Although aging may reduce productivity due to a decline in both mobility and physical fitness of older people, older smallholders leveraged their empirical knowledge to optimize input allocation, enhancing production efficiency, which had been discussed detailly in previous study [63]. Critically, older smallholders were likely to have a higher degree of collectivism from the past and exchange knowledge with others, compared to younger farmers [64, 65]. Besides, the new agricultural cooperatives assist older smallholders in enhancing yield and boosting income [56,57].

We also found that income was significantly positive with crop yield (p < 0.01), with the coefficients equal to 0.051. Also, the coefficient of agricultural machinery showed a positive relationship. It indicated that increased income and the adoption of machinery enhanced the crop yield. In order to verify the above potential causes, a more specific analysis was performed using SEM in Section 3.4.

Owing to the presence of regional heterogeneity, the demand for the production of the three main crops is subject to variation. We further divided China's provinces into eastern, central, and western regions, referring to Xu et al. [66]. Then, we analyzed the relationship between aging and cropland GHG emissions, as well as the relationship between aging and crop yield. The detailed regression coefficients were shown in Table 3. The relationship between aging and cropland GHG emissions, and aging and crop yields was not significant, expect in the western region. It showed that the older smallholders in the western region not only promote crop yield, but also reduce cropland GHG emissions. It may be contributed to that the plains dominate in the China's central and eastern regions, which are more conducive to large-scale cultivation [67]. Thus, they were less affected by aging. The western region has more smallholders, who unable to learn new knowledge and technologies timely compared to smallholders in the eastern and central regions [68]. And western regions also feature less favorable climatic conditions for crop production, characterized by higher aridity, loess soils, and undulating terrain. Thus, they were more affected by aging.

The coefficient of income in the western region was significantly higher than that in the eastern and central regions, indicating higher incomes increased cropland GHG emissions and yield. Higher-income smallholders in the western region were likely to adopt carbonintensive practices to enhance yield, such as applying more fertilizer [69]. Therefore, it is crucial to guide western smallholders towards low-carbon yield practices to achieve sustainable crop production. And the results also showed that subsidy varyingly increased cropland GHG emissions, yet its contribution to yield was negative. Therefore, it may be more effective in promoting low-carbon yield by providing actual products, such as green fertilizers [70].

Mechanization increased cropland GHG emissions and yield, especially in the central and western regions with higher mechanization. However, in the sixth column of Table 3, the coefficients were not significant and negative in the western region, which can be attributed to the mountainous terrain that limits the effectiveness of machinery [71]. Thus, providing region-specific agricultural machinery tailored to the local terrain is essential for enhancing yield in the western region, such as crawler micro rotary tiller [72].

## 3.3. Robustness check

Firstly, since cropland GHG emissions and crop yield may be affected by smallholders from different areas who controlled fertilizer application and cultivation modes, we used the instrumental variable method to overcome potential endogeneity. The average age of all samples in each province was taken as an instrumental variable, by using 65 as the division criterion. All results of F-statistic values in the first stage of Table 4 satisfied the requirements (F > 10), demonstrated that there were no weak instrumental variables. In addition, the results of endogeneity test were not significant, indicating that there were no serious endogeneity problems [44], and the results were robust.

## 3.4. Impact pathways of aging on low-carbon crop yield

To further understand how aging affected crop yield and cropland GHG emissions, SEM was used to identify the direct and indirect pathways (Fig. 4). Here, the SEM analysis revealed that only pathways with significant coefficients empirically explain the impact of aging on lowcarbon crop yield, including the paths of AGING-MACHINERY-GHG, AGING-MACHINERY-YIELD, and AGING-EDU-MACHINERY-GHG.

We found that the direct effects of aging on cropland GHG emissions and crop yield were consistent with our baseline regression result, with a standardized pathway coefficient of —0.015 (not significant) and 0.045 (0.05<P < 0.1), respectively. This indicated that the effect of aging on cropland GHG emissions and crop yield were both dominated by indirect effects, with contributions of > 90 % and 80 %, respectively.

Besides, it can be observed that the effect of the indirect impact pathway of aging on crop yield through agricultural machinery was —0.049 (-0.258×0.189). Previous study generally showed that the adoption of machinery can increase the production efficiency and crop production [76]. However, due to the low adoption rate of agricultural machinery by older smallholders, crop yields had indirectly declined. Besides, small-sized devices used by smallholders has low production efficiency [74].

Although aging was beneficial to cropland GHG emissions reduction in direct pathway, in order to ensure crop yield and efficiency, the adoption of machinery should be increased to boost production. This suggests that speeding up the research and development of agricultural machinery driven by clean energy, such as water control, land quality conservation and improvement, fertilizer and pesticide reduction and efficiency enhancement, waste resource utilization and drone seeding or spraying [77], not only can reduce cropland GHG emissions, but also increase crop yield. For instance, He et al. [77] found that with the improvement of agricultural green production technologies (AGPTs) adoption level, the agricultural carbon emissions decreased by reducing the usage of fertilizers, and it also increased yields. Liu et al. [78] found that the increased adoption rate of AGPTs led to higher yields by taking 805 citrus smallholders in Sichuan Province as a case. In addition, technical training, agricultural social services and incentives to encourage smallholders to adopt mechanized fertilization should be promoted to enhance productivity and reduce unnecessary consumption. For example, Wang and Qiu [79] found that smallholders who trained or joined farmer cooperatives, can not only use agricultural machinery more efficiently, but also contribute to emission reduction by decreasing fertilizer input. And smallholders who adopted enhanced management practices resulted in an average increase of over 10 % in crop production (rice, wheat and maize) [6].

The indirect effect of aging on cropland GHG emissions through education level and agricultural machinery was -0.014 (-0.248×0.382×0.145). This implied that the low-educated older smallholders were also reluctant to use machinery, similar to the previous finding that “high-educated farmers are earlier adopters of modern technologies" [80]. In this study, however, the average education level of the smallholder farmers was low (below high school level), which hinder the adoption of machinery. As the above analysis indicated, the adoption of mechanized fertilization had been identified as a promoting factor to increase cropland GHG emissions. Therefore, the relatively low education level decreased the machinery adoption rate, which further led to a reduction in cropland emissions through this pathway.

We also found that the effect of indirect pathway of aging on cropland GHG emissions through physical condition and farming time was negligible (0.001). In the setting of this paper, larger values of physical condition indicated poorer physical condition. The result indicated that older smallholders with poor physical condition were more likely to spend time on farming, which was contrary to the findings that “farming time increases with the improvement of health" attributing to that healthy elderly farmers generally have better farming capacity [81]. While, the possible reason for our result was that due to lacking sufficient living incomes, the older smallholders with poor physical condition had to engage in agriculture productions, which was a common phenomenon in rural China [82]. Besides, it also can be found that the effect of indirect pathway of aging on crop yield through physical condition and farming time was also negligible (0.0002).

Finally, subsidies and gender also had positive and negative effects on cropland GHG emissions, respectively, which were consistent with baseline regression results. Subsidies, which have an income effect, promoted cropland GHG emissions, in line with previous findings that farmers' income was positively correlated with fertilizer application, which is a main cropland GHG emissions contributor [84,86]. It can be inferred that subsidies promote smallholders to increase agricultural inputs, which further contributed to the increasing emissions. Besides, income also had positive significantly effects on crop yield, which was consistent with previous findings [87].

## 4. Policy implications and limitations

## 4.1. Policy implications

Valuable insights can be obtained from this study so that low-carbon crop yield of smallholders can be achieved when facing the challenges of aging.

Our results showed that subsidies and income would not only increase the adoption of machinery use, but also increase the cropland GHG emissions. Despite the resulting increase in crop yield, the relatively small machinery adopted by smallholders with high energy consumption and low efficiency can pose a challenge to low-carbon crop yield goal. And smallholders' adoption of more efficient agricultural machinery is restricted by their relatively low education level and physical deterioration as aging. To alleviate aging labor constraints and low-educated prevalent situations, it is necessary to strengthen agricultural training and optimize the availability of agricultural social services. For example, Japan had implemented effective policies such as land transfer subsidies and the accelerated development of new agricultural management systems [17]. These policies have significantly improved agricultural efficiency and attracted younger generations to farming. Given China's similar challenges of labor aging and the need to maintain food security while promoting sustainable development, these models provide valuable experience. The government should provide more opportunities for smallholders to participate in agricultural training and exchange of production experience through cooperation with enterprises and research institutions [88]. Besides, targeted subsidies should be provided to older smallholders, taking their physical condition into account and prioritizing policies in favor of older smallholders. Japan's farmer income insurance and agricultural disaster compensation systems offer excellent examples of how to compensate for income losses due to market turbulence [17]. Incentive policies can be promoted to achieve effective management in order to attract more young farmers to engage in agricultural production to mitigate aging labor constraints, such as offering agricultural training courses and providing scholarships [15,73]. In addition, Japan and South Korea have successfully promoted smart technologies like fertigation and drones, which provides further models that could be adapted to China's context [89,90]. Therefore, the development of green energy agricultural machinery and efficient agricultural technologies should be accelerated to reduce emissions while improving crop production. Finally, it is suggested that providing more financial subsidies to persons over 65 years can help purchase agricultural machinery with green energy and manure, and support for agricultural machinery rental services. For instance, in the western region, the promotion of smart agricultural machinery should be recommended to mitigate the impact by aging.

## 4.2. Limitations

Our study has some limitations that may affect the accuracy of our findings. The main objective of this study was to clarify the impact and pathways of rural labor aging on low-carbon crop yield. The time period we could acquire was only one year, however, it still comprised a sample of 2889 surveyed rural households with cultivating three major crops. Crop yield and GHG emissions can be influenced by various factors, including climate change, policy adjustments, and market price fluctuations, which can be varied with study period. This temporal variability might impact the observed relationships between the aging of the rural labor force and crop yield or GHG emissions. To address this issue, future research can cover a longer study period. Furthermore, although the research used province fixed effects to control all area characteristics that may affect aging, heterogeneity among provinces is still worth being discussed in future research. Despite these limitations, the findings of the relationship between aging and indicators affecting low-carbon crop yield still can provide insightful implications facing the challenges of aging.

## 5. Conclusions

To achieve the agricultural sustainable development goal, cropland GHG emissions mitigation and maintaining food security are crucial. Although considerable potentials of GHG mitigation and crop production have been demonstrated from optimization of production modes, management practices, technologies, etc. Rural labor aging, which can substantially impact the low-carbon crop yield, has been generally ignored. This challenge is more serious in China, where smallholders dominate crop production and the rural population aging intensifies remarkably. The study uncovered the driving mechanisms between the aging of the rural labor force and low-carbon crop yield, and constructed the methodology that can be applied to other countries facing the challenge of rural labor aging and cropland GHG emissions. We found that the effect of aging on cropland GHG emissions and crop yield were both dominated by indirect effects, with contributions > 90 % and 80 %, respectively. Aging can hinder the adoption of agricultural machinery, which can decrease the crop yield while reduce the cropland GHG emissions, especially for the low-educated smallholders. This highlighted the tradeoffs between crop yield and cropland GHG mitigation when taking the indirect effects of agricultural machinery adoption into consideration, in the face of rural labor aging. Moreover, although income and subsidies contributed to cropland GHG emissions, it increased the crop yield, suggesting the challenge to achieve synergy between the two. Finally, to overcome these challenges, policy implications including strengthening agricultural training and social services, supplying targeted subsidies, and innovating green machinery were raised.
