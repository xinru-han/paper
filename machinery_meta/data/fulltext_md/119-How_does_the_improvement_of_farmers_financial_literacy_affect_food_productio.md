# How does the improvement of farmers' financial literacy affect food production? Analysis of behavior and constraints based on element substitution and planting structure adjustment

Yichao Li1 · Xintong Xie² · Guihua Liu1 · Dingde Xu1,3

Received: 1 May 2023 / Accepted: 5 March 2024 / Published online: 20 April 2024   
© International Society for Plant Pathology and Springer Nature B.V. 2024

## Abstract

Food security is the foundation of national security, yet few studies have explored the impact of financial literacy on food production in the context of deepening rural financial development. Using data from the 2021 China Land Economy Survey, this paper theoretically and empirically analyzes the impact of factor substitution and cropping restructuring behavior on grain production brought about by farmers' financial literacy and further discusses the heterogeneous impact on grain production under different constraints. The results show that: (1) Farmers' overall financial literacy varies widely within the household, and the proportion of planting is still dominated by grain cultivation; (2) The increase of financial literacy has a significant inhibitory effect on grain cultivation area and grain mechanization; (3) Differences in the level of financial literacy and the difficulty of mechanical substitution will lead to heterogeneous effects on outcomes. The study recommends that governments should pay attention to the phenomenon of financial literacy-induced de-fooding. On the one hand, there is a need to make food production more economical and profitable for farmers; on the other hand, there is a need to regulate and advocate for comprehensive financial literacy and to reject profit-seeking.

Keywords Financial literacy ·Food production · Bartik IV · Planting structure·Advanced financial literacy

## 1 Introduction

Food security is an important foundation for national security and a necessary prerequisite for addressing risk challenges. The world food situation is not optimistic, since 2019, the number of hungry people has increased by more than 122 million globally as a result of delayed and recurring neo-crest epidemics, climatic shocks, and conflict turmoil such as the war in Ukraine. In 2022, Asia and Latin America have made progress in reducing hunger, but hunger levels are still climbing in West Asia, the Caribbean and African subregions. The

African continent continues to bear the brunt, with one in five people going hungry and more than twice the global average. If the situation is left unchecked, the world is not on track to achieve the sustainable development goal of eradicating hunger by 2030. China's grain has been stabilized at more than 1.3 trillion pounds for nine years, and the grain fundamentals are developing in a positive direction. However, the income structure of farming households has gradually changed with the development of the social economy. From mainly agricultural income to mainly non-farm income, the importance of food income for farming households has decreased, the proportion of farming households abandoning food production and switching to cash crops with higher economic value, and the proportion of abandoned farmland in rural areas has gradually increased, which has aroused thoughts from the academic and political circles. The per capita business income of rural residents since the reform and opening up percentage decreases from 84.90% in 1978 to 35.47% in 2020 according to statistics, while the wage income increases from 7.03% in 1978 to 40.70% in 2020. How to guarantee basic food security in China and even in developing countries has become the focus of urgent attention.

Food security has been the focus of academic and political attention, and most existing studies focused on the portrayal of spatial and temporal changes in food security (Ding et al., 2016; Sun et al., 2020; Wei et al., 2018), the impact of food security on farm household income (Mabuza et al., 2016; Owusu et al., 2011) and factors affecting farmers' food security (Mango et al., 2014; Reincke et al., 2018). Among them, the study of factors influencing farmers' food security is a hot topic of academic attention, and academics have explored the impact of different levels of influencing factors on farmers' food security from different perspectives, mainly including resource endowment (e.g., arable land, water resources) (Ahmed et al., 2021; Kebede et al., 2023; Liu & Huang, 2015; Sinyolo et al., 2014), technological progress (e.g., good varieties, production efficiency) (Kassie et al., 2014; Qi et al., 2002), grain growing subjects (e.g., household resource allocation, labor quality, and labor mobility) (Hamersma & Kim, 2016; Kuiper et al., 2020; Liu & Huang, 2015), and national policies (e.g., land titling, grain subsidies) (Bureau & Swinnen, 2018; Huang & Yang, 2017), which provide useful references for the conduct of this study borrowings. However, few studies have focused on the impact of financial literacy on farmers' food production.

The financial literacy of urban and rural residents has also been improving with the accelerated pace of modernization in China. Klapper et al. (2013) defined financial literacy as "the ability to process economic information and make informed decisions about financial planning, wealth accumulation, debt and pensions". Theoretically, financial literacy has both positive and negative effects on farmers' food production. On the one hand, financial literacy was a form of human capital, its enhancement can improve farmers' returns and contribute to food production (Lusardi & Mitchell, 2014). On the other hand, food production is typically cyclical, where the input of certain factors of production can be considered as an intertemporal investment, and under the assumption of "rational economic man", financial literacy is profit-seeking, and food crops with lower returns under equal cultivation may not be selected, which may threaten food production security.

So, under today's real-world conditions, does increase financial literacy have an impact on farmers' food production? If so, does it promote food production or suppress it? The answers to this series of questions require systematic theoretical sorting and solid empirical analysis. The marginal contributions of this study are: on the one hand, theoretically, we analyze the mechanism of financial literacy on farmers' food production and establish a corresponding theoretical analysis framework; on the other hand, we empirically test the direct mechanism of financial literacy on farmers' food security based on primary survey data, and fully consider the influence of factor substitution and planting structure adjustment behavior and constraints. The research findings have strong practical guidance significance.

## 2 Theoretical analysis and research hypothesis

## 2.1 The impact of financial literacy on factor inputs and cropping restructuring

The research framework of this paper is shown in Fig. 1. For farmers who do not engage in agricultural production, increased financial literacy may promote better off-farm employment or entrepreneurship for farmers (Cumurovic & Hyll, 2019; Huang et al., 2015). For farmers who continue to engage in agricultural activities, financial literacy may have two impact paths: for farmers with sufficient household endowments, increasing financial literacy may lead to moderate-scale land management through farmland transfer, thereby increasing income and reducing production cos. For farmers with constrained household endowments, financial literacy also has two possible paths of influence: first, they usually seek alternative factor inputs (e.g., machinery) when farmers' household labor is insufficient; second, they usually choose to adjust the cropping structure to maximize profits when farmers' household labor is sufficient.

In this paper, we focus on the case of farmers facing constraints in household endowments, where financial literacy affects food production through three mechanisms with constant arable land area, as shown in Fig. 2.

First, financial literacy directly affects food production. On one hand, financial literacy is often seen as a human capital element contributing to improving economic performance. On the other hand, financial literacy can directly influence farmers' financial decisions. When financial literacy is invested in food production as a production factor, it can effectively reduce financial market access (e.g., access to loans, financial management, insurance, and other services) and promote the cultivation of food crops and factor inputs through optimal resource allocation (Boatman & Evans, 2017; Huston, 2012; Sayinzoga et al., 2016); and when financial literacy is involved in farmers' cropping structure adjustment decisions, they may reduce cultivation of food crops with relatively cheaper market prices and choose more profitable cash crops instead, which eventually inhibits factor inputs of food crops.

Second, financial literacy can alleviate the liquidity constraint in agricultural production of farm households and indirectly affect food production. Financial literacy has a significant effect on farm households' income generation and can effectively alleviate farm households' liquidity constraints (Chiodi et al., 2012). In the case of limited arable land, the alleviation of liquidity constraints may have two effects on farm households. On the one hand, it affects farm households' production factor inputs. The likelihood that farmers will consider increasing farm factor inputs such as mechanization increases as the ability to pay increases. On the other hand, it may affect the farming structure adjustment of farmers. Farmers are more likely to make choices according to the liquidity sensitivity of different crops to maximize profits as liquidity increases.

Third, financial literacy enhances the labor supply constraint of farm households' agricultural activities, which indirectly affects food production. Financial literacy significantly contributed to off-farm employment of farm households, which leads to labor shortage of farm households and constrains their labor allocation in agriculture (Li et al., 2013). There are usually two ways to rationalize the allocation of production factors to maximize efficiency under limited arable land. One is to optimize the input structure of production factors. The rational use of mechanized inputs by farmers to replace human inputs can alleviate the dilemma of labor shortage or liberate labor to seek greater economic benefits. The second is to optimize the planting structure (specifically refers to adjusting the ratio of food crops to cash crops) to maximize benefits. Generally speaking, there are significant differences between food crops and cash crops in terms of factor inputs. Firstly, it is more difficult to mechanize the production of cash crops compared to food crops. Secondly, the effect of inputs and outputs is different. Food crops, as land-intensive products, have lower input costs and lower output returns, but are easier to achieve large-scale; cash crops, such as vegetables and fruits, are high-added labor-intensive products with higher input costs but also high output returns. Finally, there is the issue of labor utilization. Food crops are cyclical and seasonal, and food growers are "farming in the busy season and working in the idle season" with obvious part-time characteristics; most cash crops can be produced throughout the year, so the labor utilization rate is high and the whole family can be more fully employed throughout the year.

Based on the above analysis, for most farmers facing household endowment constraints, financial literacy acts on food production through multiple paths. In terms of cropping structure, financially literate farmers are usually able to calculate the costs and benefits of agricultural production and make cropping decisions based on the principle of profit maximization. Theoretically, most cash crops are characterized by high inputs and high returns, and thus have larger profit margins and are more sensitive to liquidity than food crops, conditions that may discourage food crop cultivation. However, the reality is that farming decisions can be influenced by conditions such as topography, input costs, and whether or not they are part-time, and farmers can be left with land that is unavailable for cash crops, unaffordable capital costs for cash crops and too little labor to grow cash crops, which may lead to financial literacy promoting food crop cultivation.

In terms of factor inputs, financially literate farmers usually acquire skills that facilitate farmers' access to credit and promote wealth accumulation, such as access to production funds, liquid asset allocation, and financial income and expenditure management, thus enhancing the ability and willingness to invest in agricultural production. In the theory, financial literacy will drive labor-starved farmers to adopt machinery, and on the other hand, financial literacy directly serves as human capital input and indirectly relieves liquidity, which makes farmers have higher payment ability and increase the possibility of adopting mechanization; in reality, the arable land area usually determines the cost of machinery input, and most farmers do not operate arable land area enough to generate scale effect, so farmers may not choose to adopt mechanization. However, for a small proportion of farmers with a larger scale of operation, the cost of mechanization is relatively small due to the higher returns to scale brought by mechanical inputs, so these farmers will prefer to adopt mechanization for food crop cultivation. Based on the above analysis, two sets of hypotheses are proposed:

H1a: Financial literacy will promote food production and discourage cash crop production for farming households facing household endowment constraints.

H1b: Financial literacy will discourage food production and promote cash crop production for farming households facing household endowment constraints.

H2a: Financial literacy will deter farmers from substituting factors of food production (reducing machinery inputs)

H2b: Financial literacy will promote food production factor substitution (increase machinery inputs) for farming households facing household endowment constraints.

## 2.2 The impact of financial literacy on factor inputs and cropping restructuring under specific terrain constraints

In reality, both of the above structural adjustments will be limited by specific conditions. When farmers' household labor is insufficient, farmers will tend to choose mechanized farming, and then mechanical alternative factor inputs need to face the constraints of the difficulty of factor substitution, and usually, the terrain barrier effect will reduce the operability of farm machinery operation (Zhou et al., 2013), so the terrain condition is an important determinant of arable land mechanization. Generally, plain areas are suitable for the mechanization of arable land operations, while in hilly and mountainous areas, agricultural mechanization is more difficult. Therefore, the impact of structural adjustment strategies adopted by farm households under different conditions on food cultivation areas will be different.

Mechanization is affected by topography, and financial literacy may not play a significant role in hilly and mountainous areas where mechanization is more difficult and productive services are less available, and where arable land is more commonly cultivated by farmers themselves. Instead, in the plains, where mechanization is less difficult to replace, financial literacy will play a key role in farmers' cropping decisions. Based on this, hypothesis H3 is proposed.

H3: The inhibitory effect of farmers' financial literacy on food production is influenced by the difficulty of factor substitution. On arable land in hilly and mountainous areas, the less easy it is for machinery to replace labor, the inhibitory effect of financial literacy on food production is not significant. However, on the plains, financial literacy significantly inhibits food production.

## 2.3 The impact of initial and advanced financial literacy on food production

Financial literacy usually plays a role in crop-growing decisions and its behavior follows the profit maximization principle. The investment threshold for cash crops has been lowered due to the considerable return on investment and the increasingly mature technology chain. Cash crops become a better investment than food crops, and therefore, farmers with higher financial literacy are relatively more likely to choose cash crops:

H4: Farmers with advanced financial literacy are more likely to reject growing food crops than those with primary financial literacy.

## 3 Data and methods

## 3.1 Data source

The data used in this study are mainly from the China Land Economy Survey (CLES) 2021 national survey database of Nanjing Agricultural University. The survey method is face-to-face interviews. The format of the interview is a structured interview. According to the predesigned questionnaire, the investigators ask questions and the farmer answer. The survey covers the production structure, factor inputs and financial literacy of rural households. Each questionnaire lasts about 2 h. Some of these objective indicators, such as household labor size, mainly ask about the status of rural households as of the end of 2020. And some subjective indicators, such as financial concern, which mainly ask respondents about their status at the time of the interview. The CLES has a special "financial literacy panel", which can clearly distinguish the primary financial literacy and the advanced financial literacy of the subjects. In this study, probability proportionate to size sampling (PPS) was used to determine the survey sample to ensure the representativeness of the selected sample in the survey. The process is as follows:

First, all districts of 13 cities in Jiangsu Province were selected using unequal probability sampling based on the rural population in the 2010 census of each district as a proportion of the rural population of the city to which they belonged, and two research districts and counties were selected for each city. Secondly, the number of villages in each township and town street was based on the proportion of the sample counties, and unequal probability sampling was used to draw two town streets in each county. Each town took 1 village, and finally, we got 52 administrative villages in 24 counties of 12 cities in Jiangsu Province.

According to the above process, a total of 2600 valid rural household survey questionnaires were obtained. This study was matched to the 2020 CLES financial literacy data and a final sample of 1642 valid samples was obtained by removing missing and distorted samples for key variables in the data cleaning.

## 3.2 Variable description

Three explanatory variables were selected to characterize farmers' planting and machinery factor inputs (Zhong et al., 2016), including the proportion of cultivated area of grain, the average machinery input per 666.667 m² of grain crops, and the machinery input ratio of grain crops. In this questionnaire, farmers answered all the crops they planted (up to six) to characterize their total cultivated area, including five food crops (wheat, rice, corn, soybean, and potato) and ten cash crops (cotton, rape, peanut, sunflower, sesame, vegetable, apple, pear, peach, and grape). Each crop answered by farmers has corresponding input costs and expenses, and this paper uses "machinery operation cost" to represent the machinery input of a crop and "self-invested man-days" to represent the labor input of a crop.

The OECD definition of financial literacy is the most widely used and defines financial literacy as the combination of awareness, knowledge, skills, attitudes, and behaviors required to make sound financial decisions and ultimately achieve one's financial well-being, mainly including financial knowledge, financial attitudes, and financial behaviors.

To explore the heterogeneous impact of financial literacy, this paper further divides financial literacy into primary financial literacy and advanced financial literacy (Van Rooij et al., 2011). Financial literacy refers to an individual's ability to understand finance-related terms and issues; primary financial literacy refers to an individual's understanding of simple interest, compound interest, inflation, and the time value of money, while advanced financial literacy refers to an individual's mastery of more complex financial knowledge related to stocks, portfolios, etc. This paper uses five questions to measure the financial literacy of the respondents as shown in Table 1.

In Eq. (1), the explanatory variable Y, denotes the farming behavior decision of farm household i including the proportion of area planted with grain and the input of machinery factors. The parameters in Eq. (1) were estimated using the least squares method. However, the study may face endogenous challenges. On the one hand, the results of this regression may be affected by omitted variable bias such as financial inclusion and risk attitudes. On the other hand, the results may also lead to the problem of reverse causality, i.e., changes in cropping structure can also change the financial literacy of farmers. For example, farmers who grow apples may pay more attention to apple-related stocks.

To address the endogeneity issue, this paper reconstructs the "Bartik instrument" variable. Bartik IV can be a good solution to endogeneity problems due to omitted variables and reverse causation (Derenoncourt, 2022). Drawing on Bartik (2009) and

Yi and Zhou (2018), this paper constructs the "Bartik instrument", which is the lagged first-order multiplication of the financial literacy of farm households by the first-order difference in financial literacy over time in the sample provinces.

## 4.3 Robustness checks

In order to test the robustness of the estimation results, two methods are used in this paper (Li & Qian, 2020). One is that the financial literacy indicator using the score summation method, i.e., the score is calculated by the number of questions in which the household correctly answered the five questions mentioned in the previous section, and one point is given for each correct answer (where the fifth question is considered to be "1", "2" and "3" were regarded as having financial information sensitivity, and one point was added; farmers who answered "4" and "5" were regarded as not having financial information (No additional points for "4" and "5"). Second, the instrumental variable is replaced with the regression of the average financial literacy level in the county. County-level indicators are more exogenous to individual farmers and are less likely to be correlated with the disturbance term. The results are consistent with the previous estimates and represent the robustness of the results in this paper (Tables 6 and 7).

## 5 Discussion

There are three possible points of contribution to this study. First, it breaks down research boundaries. Previous research on financial literacy is usually linked to areas such as investment and consumption (Grohmann et al., 2018), and there is little literature related to the discussion of financial literacy and agricultural production. The core dependent variable in this paper is the percentage of area under food cultivation, which is a more realistic indicator as it measures both the area under food cultivation and the cultivation choices of farmers. In addition, machinery inputs and self-invested labor days are two indicators, they further reflect the corresponding changes in labor factors of production after farmers make planting structure adjustments, this indicator is also realistic. Second, in terms of research data, this paper selects representative micro data to reflect the problem. Microdata comes from face-to-face interviews, which better reflects the source of the problem fundamentally, and is very suitable for eliciting the actual problem. To a certain extent, the conclusions of this paper are the real problems faced by farmers. Third, the paper conducts a targeted heterogeneity analysis. The study measures the heterogeneous role of junior and senior financial literacy and whether or not plains on food production in terms of heterogeneity in financial literacy and heterogeneity in topography, respectively. The findings can enrich existing research and also provide more specific directions for policy makers.

This study has both similarities and differences with previous studies. The similarities are, first, the selection of indicators in this study are from existing literature. The constructional aspects of the financial literacy index, from the design of the questions to the assignment of the responses, are derived from the existing literature (Lusardi & Mitchell, 2007). Second, it draws on well-established theoretical thinking. Food production and financial literacy, as two fields, have developed relatively wellestablished logical theories, and this paper also stands on the shoulders of its predecessors to see the world. The differences are, first, the innovation of the question of financial literacy. In this paper, based on the original literature, the question on the choice of farmers' planting is added, which makes the whole index closer to the study of this paper. The second is the breaking of scientific margins. This paper breaks the barrier between the two fields and makes innovative thinking in it. Food can be regarded as an investment good, and financial literacy can also be regarded as human capital and factors of production, thus forming a rigorous logical closed loop. Thirdly and most importantly, the paper discusses issues on a practical basis. In reality, advanced financial literacy may be a development trend, and the paper further discusses the possible impact of more advanced financial literacy as a wake-up call to the world that development can also have profound negative impacts.

Although the data in this study are from China, they reflect to some extent the problems of developing countries. As economic globalization deepens, so does the financial literacy of the world's farmers. For developing countries in particular, farmers face pressures on their own agriculture as well as potential shocks from international markets, resulting in varying degrees of "de-fooding" in many countries (Chauvin et al., 2012). Based on their desire for a better life, farmers have always adjusted their planting decisions in the hope of obtaining greater returns. Financial literacy, as a composite indicator, largely reflects the degree of profit-seeking among farmers. Conditions permitting, as financial literacy deepens, farmers will be more inclined to grow more profitable crops or even abandon agricultural production. These phenomena are already common in developing countries (Despommier, 2010; Triplett & Dick, 2008). Therefore, the findings of this paper may be instructive for other developing countries. Finally, there are some shortcomings in this paper. First, this paper uses cross-sectional data, which means that it increases the difficulty of causality identification. This cannot be improved due to the availability of data. In the subsequent study, it can be based on panel data and macro data in order to test the trend and length of financial literacy on food production. Secondly, although this paper constructs financial literacy based on classical literature. there are inevitable biases (Lusardi & Mitchell, 2011). If more indicators are available and financial literacy is measured more carefully in subsequent studies, different findings may be found. Finally, the paper is based on a linear model for discussion and the results are monotonic. Whether there is a non-linear relationship between financial literacy on food production needs to be further verified. The good and bad development of affairs are relative, and subsequent studies can be validated by more nonlinear methods with a view to finding the inflection point of development, which will have more policy implications.

## 6 Conclusions and policy recommendations

Based on the above analysis, we can get the following conclusions:

1. At this stage, Chinese farmers are still mainly graingrowing type, and the overall financial literacy within farmers varies greatly.

2. The increase of financial literacy has a significant inhibitory effect on grain production. Financial literacy has a significant dampening effect on food production, as evidenced by the fact that financial literacy dampens the acreage planted with food, as well as labor and machinery inputs for food.

3. Heterogeneity analysis showed that advanced financial literacy had a greater inhibitory effect on food production than primary financial literacy. The inhibitory effect of financial literacy on food production was more significant in the plains than in the non-plains.

In the context of the rapid development of rural finance, how will the improvement of farmers' financial literacy affect food security? The findings of this paper help the government to scientifically study the relationship between the two and formulate a scientific and reasonable food development strategy, with specific policy implications:

1. At the theoretical level, the government should include financial literacy in the category of testing indicators. The conclusions of this paper directly enrich the existing factors affecting food security and provide a new research horizon for the academic community. In particular, as food security is the fundamental security of a country, countries should establish corresponding early warning mechanisms, such as the "red line" of 1.8 billion mu of arable land proposed by China. In the context of globalization, financial literacy, as an important factor directly affecting food security, should be taken seriously. It should be included in the testing indicators to ensure that it could be jeopardized.

2. In practical terms, the practical application should be alerted to the trend of de-fooding that financial literacy has already created and should be guided in a timely manner. Empirical results confirm that financial literacy has jeopardized food production. Therefore, it is particularly important to ensure that financial literacy is on the upswing. The government should increase its efforts to publicize the importance of food so that farmers can develop the right values; on the other hand, it should also make farmers feel that it is really profitable so that the countryside can be transformed into a real pole of growth.

3. In the context of changing labor costs, food production in different regions will be heterogeneous, and the government should develop different strategies according to local conditions to create good conditions for farmers. In plain areas, further reduction of mechanization cost will be the primary issue. Although the plains are suitable for growing food crops, high-yield cash crops are more likely to be the farming choice of farmers than food crops that cannot reduce production costs. Therefore, the government should seek cheaper mechanization and socialization services in the plains. On the one hand, it can adopt "group purchase" outsourcing services, starting from the consumer side, the mechanized process of weeding, harvesting, pesticide, etc. will be split up and sold, on the other hand, starting from the production side, reduce the production cost of the machinery itself or improve the productivity of the machinery to reduce the price. In hilly mountainous areas, labor becomes an important constraint due to the high difficulty of mechanization of farming. Cash crops will bring higher returns, but they also require more labor, and farmers with insufficient family labor can only choose to grow food crops. Therefore, the government needs to innovate machinery that can operate in hilly mountainous areas to promote food production.
