## ABSTRACT

Agricultural mechanization services (AMS) are important for promoting sustainable agricultural development. However, little is known about the effect of outsourcing fertilizer mechanization services (FMS) for fertilizer application to different types of service providers. This study simultaneously investigates the determinants and effect of outsourcing FMS to different types of service providers for fertilizer application in rural China. The multinomial endogenous treatment effects model that accounts for potential selection bias and endogeneity is employed to analyze cross-sectional survey data of wheat producers. The results reveal that the likelihood of outsourcing FMS is influenced by household (gender, education, off-farm employment, and risk attitude) and farm (farm size, land topography, soil fertility) characteristics, membership to cooperative, and the availability of AMS at village level. The treatment effect estimates suggest that outsourcing FMS can decrease fertilizer application by at least 6.6%. Outsourcing both FMS and fertilizer supply has a greater reducing effect on fertilizer application compared to only outsourcing FMS. Besides, outsourcing both FMS and fertilizer supply to organization providers has a higher effect on reducing fertilizer application and enhancing yield compared to outsourcing to individual service providers. The results have policy implications for promoting agri-environmental sustainability in the agricultural sector.

## 1 | Introduction

Chemical fertilizers are crucial in modern agricultural production. The widespread application of fertilizer has greatly contributed to substantial increase in global grain production (Stewart et al. 2005). However, it has also come with excessive use of chemical fertilizers (Huang and Jiang 2019; Zhu and

Chen 2002), mainly due to inappropriate fertilizer management practices (Smith and Siciliano 2015). Empirical studies show that fertilizer management practice in China is influenced by farm size, availability of agricultural labor, age of the labor force, and agronomic knowledge and skills of farm operators (Ju et al. 2016; Zhu et al. 2022). Excessive fertilizer application is generally associated with water pollution (rivers, groundwater), heavy metals, and greenhouse emissions, which are increasingly becoming a pervasive global problem (Bijay-Singh and Craswell 2021). The extent of potential environmental damage depends on the rate of fertilizer application per acre and the total acres farmed. Thus, reducing excessive application of fertilizer is associated with positive environmental outcomes.

Numerous empirical studies have investigated factors influencing fertilizer application at the farm level (Guo et al. 2021; Ma et al. 2018; Zhang et al. 2020; Zhou et al. 2010). However, few studies have investigated the effect of outsourcing fertilizer mechanization services (FMS) to different types of providers on the rate of fertilizer application. The emergence of AMS rental market has made it possible for many agricultural producers to access agricultural machinery without the need of ownership (Belton et al. 2021; Yang et al. 2013). This is crucial for smallholder producers who cannot afford the initial high capital investment required to own machinery. Farmers may choose to outsource some or all of their machinery needs to individual or organization machinery service providers to alleviate the adverse effects of labor shortages and high cost of labor (Afridi et al. 2023; Gallardo and Sauer 2018; Hamilton et al. 2022; Wang et al. 2016; Zhang et al. 2017). Outsourcing mechanization services can substitute for agricultural labor (Meng et al. 2024), which enables farmers to expand their scale of operations and increase agricultural productivity. Fertilizer mechanical service providers often employ agricultural machinery with advanced technology and have technical expertise to determine the appropriate rate of fertilizer application compared to farmers, helping address farmers' inadequacies of knowledge and skills on fertilizer management.

Unbalanced fertilizer use is a major reason for crop variable yield (Islam et al. 2024). Compared to conventional manual fertilizer application done by farmers, FMS providers can use variable rate technology to spread fertilizer, thereby promoting fertilizer use efficiency. The deep placement of fertilizer in the soil optimizes crop yields while minimizing environmental damage (Zhu et al. 2024). Thus, there is an urgent need to investigate the nexus between FMS and fertilizer application. There are four options of FMS that farmers in rural China can outsource fertilizer management to services providers. First, farmers can buy chemical fertilizers from local markets and outsource FMS to individual service providers, such as neighbors. Second, farmers can buy chemical fertilizers from local markets but outsource fertilizer application machinery services to organization service providers such as cooperatives and agricultural companies. In the two cases, farmers only hire machinery for at least one fertilizer application. Third, farmers can outsource both FMS and fertilizer supply to individual service providers. Fourth, farmers can outsource both FMS and fertilizer supply to organization service providers. Outsourcing FMS ensures more accurate and uniform spread of fertilizers across the wheat fields. When farmers outsource both FMS and fertilizer supply, service providers can optimize fertilizer use based on the specific conditions of the soil, reducing the risk of excessive application of fertilizer while maintaining crop yield. The individual and organizational service providers often differ in their levels of technical expertise and machinery types, with the latter using machinery with more advanced technology which ensures higher precision and effectiveness in fertilizer application compared to the former. However, little is known on how the rate of fertilizer application differs depending on the FMS providers outsourced to apply fertilizer.

Previous studies have investigated factors that influence farmers' decision to outsource AMS and the role of AMS in enhancing farm production in developing countries (e.g., Deng et al. 2020; Ghosh 2010; Huan et al. 2022; Ji et al. 2017; Kirui 2019; Qiu et al. 2021; Takeshima 2017). Decision to outsourcing AMS is significantly influenced by several factors, including farm's scale of operation, the cost of outsourcing, income from off-farm employment, and government support through subsidies (Wang et al. 2020). In addition, outsourcing mechanization services reduces the likelihood of smallholders exiting farming (Qiu et al. 2021) and the intensity of agricultural labor (Mi et al. 2020), leading to an expansion of cultivated cropland in Africa and Nepal (Kirui 2019; Takeshima 2017). It has also been found to increase crop yield and farm income in Tanzania (Kotu et al. 2023), and farm efficiency in China (Deng et al. 2020; Huan et al. 2022).

Agricultural mechanical services not only increase land productivity, but it is also effective in achieving long-term environmental sustainability. AMS significantly promotes farmers adoption of no-tillage technology and organic fertilizer application technology (Qing et al. 2023). With the expansion of the contiguous farm scale due to agricultural mechanization, the effect of AMS on reducing fertilizer application is significant (Zhang et al. 2022). The effect of outsourcing AMS on reducing fertilizer application quantity is related to land topography; it is more effective in plain areas compared to hilly and mountainous areas (Chen et al. 2023). Different AMS providers exert varying effects on fertilizer application. Outsourcing fertilization services reduces fertilizer application and frequency among part-time farmers in China (Xia and Cui 2023; Zheng et al. 2023). Furthermore, outsourcing both FMS and fertilizer supply is more effective in reducing fertilizer application compared to outsourcing FMS only among corn farmers in Northeast China (Liu et al. 2022). In general, few studies have investigated the impact of outsourcing mechanization services on fertilizer application. Furthermore, although these studies looked at how outsourcing AMS affect fertilizer application, they only focused on outsourcing AMS services in general and did not consider the effect of outsourcing FMS to diverse types of service providers on fertilizer application.

This study makes three important contributions to the emerging body of literature on agricultural mechanization and outsourcing services. First, the existing literature focuses on general outsourcing services while this study specifically focuses on fertilizer mechanical services. Second, compared with previous studies, we consider two types of service providers, the individual and the organization providers. We also consider two cases; the case where the farmer only outsources fertilizer mechanization services but purchases their own fertilizer from the market. The other case is where they outsource both fertilizer mechanization services and fertilizer supply to the service provider. Third, our study simultaneously analyzes the determinants of the choice of the varying types of fertilizer mechanization services on the quantity of applied fertilizer using a multinomial endogenous treatment effects model. Our results shed light on how the choice of FMS providers and type of service affect sustainable agricultural practices. Our findings have policy implications that can be extended to other developing countries to promote sustainable agricultural production.

This study is structured as follows. Section 2 presents the background and data. Section 3 presents the theoretical framework. Section 4 presents the econometric approach. The results are presented and discussed in Section 5. Section 6 concludes with a summary of the main findings, and policy implications.

## 2 | Backgroundand Data

## 2.1 | Background

To achieve the United Nations' Sustainable Development Goals of no poverty (SDG-1) and zero hunger (SDG-2), the agriculture sector must increase food production to feed the increasing global population while reducing negative environmental impact. China the world's second most populous country can play a crucial role in stabilizing the global food security by ensuring food security for its more than 1.4 billion people.

However, China faces constraints in advancing sustainable agricultural development. First, the reduction and aging of the agricultural labor are on the rise. The percentage of the population employed in the primary sector dropped from 50% in 2000% to 22.80% in 2023 (CNBS, 2024) (Figure 1), and the rural elderly population aged 60 and above accounted for 23.8% of the total rural population in 2020. Lack of sufficient skilled agricultural labor hurts agricultural production. Second, over 98% of agricultural operators are still smallholders, managing around 70% of the total cultivated land area, and approximately 85.2% of households cultivate less than 10 mu (0.67 ha)¹. Expanding farm operational scale through large-scale land consolidation remains impractical due to high transaction costs and expectations of rising land prices, which hinder land transfers. Third, the country is facing environmental degradation caused by excessive use of chemical inputs. The extensive application of chemical fertilizers has significantly boosted agricultural production but also led to severe environmental pollution. Fertilizer usage for grain crops has surpassed the recommended optimal levels (Chou et al. 2014; Kong et al. 2018; Xin 2022), which urgently needs to be changed.

Outsourcing of AMS has enabled farmers to consolidate fragmented plots and expand their scale of operations without land transfer. This has promoted regional cultivation of staple crops such as grains. It has also enabled the use of green and efficient production technologies, resulting in increased grain yields and farm incomes. In recent years, the Chinese government has vigorously promoted the development of AMS. The central government's financial support for agricultural socialized services has increased from 500 million yuan in 2013 to 8.8 billion yuan in 2024. The number of AMS providers increased from 5.25 million in 2000 to 7.87 million in 2021, with the proportion of agricultural mechanization service organizations among total agricultural mechanization service providers rising from 22.05% in 2000 to 26.58% in 2021. The number of individual service providers increased from 4.09 million in 2000 to 5.78 million in 2021. Within agricultural mechanization service organizations, the proportion of members belonging to agricultural mechanization cooperatives grew to 68.54% in 2021².

As the world's largest producer of wheat, China produced 17.28% of the world's wheat output in 2023 (Figure 2). Although the total land area used for wheat production decreased from 26.65 million hectares in 2000 to 23.09 million hectares in 2024, total wheat output increased from 99.64 million tonnes to 138.22 million tonnes during the same period (CNBS,2024). The significant role of AMS in boosting agricultural production is widely documented (Lu et al. 2022; Qiu et al. 2021; Qing et al. 2019; Zhou et al. 2020). Agricultural mechanization plays an important role in determining wheat production (Zhang et al. 2017). As part of the production decision, farmers can choose to use agricultural mechanization services for one or more tasks while retaining their land operation rights, allowing them to expand their scale of operation and reduce the productivity gaps.

## 2.2  Data

The data used in this study came from a 2022 rural survey on wheat farmers in the Shandong, Hebei, Henan, and Shanxi provinces of the northern region of China. The research provinces were selected for the following reasons: First, wheat is a primary grain crop in China, and according to the data from the China Rural Statistic Yearbook, wheat production constitutes approximately 19.64% of the country's total grain output in 2023 (CNBS, 2024). The four provinces are the main wheat production regions. In 2021, wheat cultivated area in Shandong, Hebei, Henan and Shanxi was 3.99, 2.25, 5.69, and 0.96 million hectares, respectively, accounting for about 54.68 percent of China's total wheat cultivation area and wheat yield was 26.37, 14.69, 38.03, and 4.25 million tons, accounting for about 60.85 per cent of China's total wheat production (CRSY 2022). Second, the whole production process of wheat is simple and easier to mechanize compared to other food crops. According to China Agricultural Machinery Industry Yearbook, the integrated mechanization rate of wheat plowing, sowing, and harvesting exceeded 97.55%, higher than that of other food crops. But the mechanization level of fertilizer application is still relatively low, which is challenging to meet the needs of modern agricultural development.

A stratified random sampling method was employed to collect the data. First, one to three counties were randomly selected from each province, considering the government financial support provided to development of agricultural mechanization services, and the structure of the agricultural industries. Second, the level of economic development in a region is important for finding off-farm work. Farmers in regions with well-developed local industries are more likely to find off-farm work and improve their household income. Therefore, two to six townships were randomly selected from each county based on different levels of their per capita industrial output value as identified in the China County Statistical Yearbook. Thereafter, four to six villages are randomly selected in each town and 12 to 20 wheat farmers were surveyed in each village. The number of sampled farmers is related to village size. To minimize potential sample bias, the questionnaires were pilot-tested and revised to reduce measurement error. After rigorous quality checks, the final dataset included 993 farm households, ensuring greater representativeness and robustness against spatial, economic, and attrition biases.

The collected information referred to the 2021 production conditions. To ensure the quality of the survey data, we conducted several trainings for the enumerators before the formal survey and explained relevant questions in detail so that they understood the detailed structured questionnaire. Face-to-face interviews were conducted by enumerators who spoke both mandarin and local dialects. The survey content included the characteristics of farm households and the farms, agricultural production data, agricultural mechanization services, village characteristics, and other contextual information.

## 2.3 | Description of Variables

Wheat production requires the application of both basal fertilizer and top-dressing fertilizer. Farmers can choose to outsource FMS for basal fertilizer and/or top-dressing fertilizer. Thus, the outcome variables of interest in this study are the quantity of fertilizer applied per mu and wheat yield per mu in wheat production. The main independent variable is outsourcing of FMS. This is a binary variable that takes a value of 1 if farmers outsource FMS to a given type of provider and zero otherwise. Based on existing empirical studies (Chang and Mishra 2012; Lu et al. 2022; Sun et al. 2018), the control variables include household head characteristics (gender, age, education, off-farm employment, and risk attitude, training), farm characteristics and household endowments (farm size, land topography, soil fertility, irrigation condition, household labor, income), and membership to cooperative. The summary statistics of the variables used in the econometric analysis are presented in Table 1. The statistics reveals that the average age of sampled household heads is 57 years, highlighting the critical issue of aging agricultural labor. About 62% of farm households engage in off-farm employment, reflecting a significant shift in rural labour dynamics. The average wheat farmland managed by sampled households is less than 1 hectare. The small farm size presents significant challenges for achieving economies of scale in agricultural production, particularly in regions where smallholder farmers predominate. Such small-scale operations can hinder the adoption of advanced technologies and mechanized services, which often require a larger operational base to be economically viable. In turn, these constraints can lead to lower productivity and less efficient resource use, particularly in the application of inputs like fertilizers.

## 4 | Econometric Approach

This study aims to analyze the effect of outsourcing the FMS to different types of service providers. We employ the multinomial endogenous treatment effects model (METE) to analyze the effects because it can handle more than two treatment variables simultaneously and accounts for both the interdependence of the choice decisions and the inherent selection bias from observed and unobserved characteristics (Deb and Trivedi 2006). The METE is estimated in two stages. In the first stage, a household chooses one of the five choices (where the control group is the farmers who do not outsource FMS). The model estimates the multinomial probability of choosing any of the four options with the no outsourcing of FMS as the base using the mixed multinomial logit (MMNL) selection model. In the second stage, ordinary least squares regression (OLS) with selectivity correction is used to estimate treatment effect of outsourcing FMS for fertilizer application.

We assume that a household will select one of the five choices to maximize anticipated benefits. Let U\*k indicate the indirect utility that the household would obtain by selecting the kth FMS, k = 0,1, ..., 4 for household h:

Although in principle the parameters of the model are identified even if qhk are identical to Xh, Deb and Trivedi (2006) recommend the use of exclusion restrictions or instruments for improving identification. Following previous studies, we use the development of rental markets for agricultural mechanization service at the village level as an instrumental variable (Hong 2019; Luan et al. 2022). This represents whether the village has organizations that provide FMS, which is a binary variable that takes on a value of one if there was at least one organization service provider and zero otherwise. Firstly, the emergency of AMS organizations increases farmers' access to outsourcing FMS, even though some farmers may still obtain these services from organizations in neighboring villages. Secondly, the availability of AMS can affect smallholder farmers' fertilizer application only through the provision of FMS (Zhao et al. 2025). While AMS development may coincide with broader agricultural modernization policies, its direct impact on fertilizer use is assumed to operate mainly through the facilitation of mechanized fertilization services. The availability of AMS at village level has been used as valid instrumental variables in prior studies (Luan et al. 2022; Zhao et al. 2025) (the instrumental variable tests are shown in the attached Appendix Table A1). This largely mitigates the endogeneity problem.

## 5 | Results and Discussion

## 5.1 | Determinants of Outsourcing Different FMS Combinations

Table 2 presents the parameter estimates of the MMNL model from the first stage of the METE model. We conducted a diagnostic test to ascertain if the MMNL model is fit for our analysis. The Wald test (χ² = 938.91; p = 0.000) rejected the hypothesis that all the regression coefficients are jointly equal to zero. This result suggests that the estimated MMNL fits the data very well.

The results show that the likelihood of outsourcing FMS for fertilizer management is significantly influenced by several factors, such as the gender, education level and risk attitude of the household head, off-farm employment, farm size, land topography, plot soil fertility, membership to cooperative, and the availability of AMS at village level.

The gender of household heads affects the decision to outsource FMS. Our results show that men are more likely to outsource FMS to organization providers and both FMS and fertilizer supply to individual providers. Compared to women, men tend to have better knowledge of fertilization machinery and organization providers often have more advanced fertilization machinery than individual providers. When faced with decisions about outsourcing FMS and fertilizer supply, men are generally more inclined to choose individual providers from their own or neighboring villages, with whom they have established familiarity and a higher level of trust. Educated producers are less likely to outsource FMS and fertilizer supply to individual providers. Households with lower education are more likely to outsourcing FMS and fertilizer supply to individuals. A possible reason is that they lack fertilizer management skills and hope to get support from FMS providers and individual service providers are easier for them to reach.

Having off-farm employment increases the likelihood of outsourcing FMS (Ch₁. Ch2) but has a positive but no significant association with outsourcing both FMS and fertilizer supply (Ch3, Ch4). There are two reasonable explanations for this. First, for households with off-farm employment, agriculture income contributes less to their overall household income, and they have a higher demand for direct labor-saving services. FMS are sufficient to meet their needs. The second explanation is that they may not fully recognize the potential benefits and advantages of outsourcing both FMS and fertilizer supply. The coefficient of farmers' risk attitude is positive and statistically significant. Our result suggests that farmers with a high degree of risk preference are more likely to outsource FMS to organization providers (Ch2, Ch4). Doing this may involve more risk than outsourcing to individual providers who may be known to farmers. Trust in FMS providers and uncertainty regarding the quality of services to be provided directly affect farmers' outsourcing decisions. Risk-tolerant farmers, who exhibit a higher degree of risk preference, are more capable of handling the uncertainties inherent in outsourcing FMS. They are more likely to believe in the professional expertise of organization service providers, viewing outsourced FMS as a means to enhance production efficiency, mitigate the risks stemming from technological complexity, and ultimately boost agricultural output.

Farm size plays an important role in outsourcing FMS decisions. Our results suggest that households operating larger plots of cultivated land are more likely to outsource FMS compared to those operating smaller land parcels. As the farm size expands, households face constraints in both labor and capital resources. The acquisition of fertilizer machinery entails substantial sunk costs, leading to an increased demand for FMS. This is consistent with the findings of prior studies in China that farm size has a significant positive impact on outsourcing AMS (Ji et al. 2017; Sun et al. 2018). Farm cooperatives membership is also an important factor in outsourcing FMS decisions. The parameter estimates show that households who are members of farm cooperatives are more likely to outsourcing FMS to organizations (Ch₂ and Ch4). As a member of farm cooperatives, households obtain more information related to FMS and easy access to FMS from cooperatives.

Farmland topography and soil fertility have a positive and significant influence on the outsourcing FMS to individual providers. This suggests that flat land provides favorable conditions for the operation of machinery and farmers who cultivate fertile soil are more likely to outsource FMS, which is a means of promoting the efficient application of agricultural technologies and help maintain or enhance soil fertility (Ma et al. 2018b).

## 5.2 | The Effects of Choosing Different FMS and FMS Providers on Fertilizer Application

Table 3 summarizes the average treatment effects of outsourcing FMS for fertilizer application. For comparison purposes, we estimate the model under the assumption of both exogenous and endogenous decisions to outsource FMS.

The upper part of Table 3 reports the OLS estimates. Based on the exogenous assumption, the results show that compared to non-outsourcing households, outsourcing FMS on average decreases fertilizer applications by 4.5 percent to 10.2 percent. As noted earlier, the OLS estimates can lead to misleading causal inferences on the impact of outsourcing FMS based on the exogenous assumption if we neglect the impact of unobserved confounding factors such as households' management abilities. Therefore, the study estimates the METE model.

The lower part of Table 3 presents the estimate after controlling for unobserved heterogeneity. The factor loading (η) shows evidence of positive selection bias of outsourcing FMS to individuals (Ch₁) or FMS and fertilizer supply to organization providers (Ch4) which suggests that the unobserved variables increase the likelihood of outsourcing FMS to individual providers or outsourcing FMS and fertilizer supply to service organization providers, which are associated with high level of fertilizer application. Alternatively, the negative selection bias for outsourcing FMS to service organizations (Ch2) or outsourcing FMS and fertilizer supply to individual providers (Ch3) suggests that unobserved factors that increase the likelihood of outsourcing FMS to service organizations or FMS and fertilizer supply to individual providers are associated with low level of fertilizer applications.

The results show that in general, outsourcing FMS and fertilizer supply reduces fertilizer applications by 6.6 percent to 21.9 percent compared with non-outsourcing FMS (the full results are attached Appendix Table A2). Specifically, outsourcing FMS to individual providers (Ch₁) has a significant reduction effect on fertilizer application compared with non-outsourcing FMS farmers. A possible explanation is that mechanized fertilizer application can effectively avoid the non-standard and uneven problem caused by manual fertilization and improve fertilizer use efficiency (Chen et al. 2023). Another explanation is that most of individual service providers come from the same village or neighboring villages and most of the households often receive information about fertilizer application through communication with their peers, who may be individual service providers. Through this exchange, knowledge about fertilization is conveyed to the farmers, promoting more rational fertilizer input use.

Outsourcing FMS and fertilizer supply (Ch3, Ch4) significantly reduces fertilizer applications compared with non-outsourcing FMS. One possible explanation for this is that service providers leverage their agricultural production experience to achieve precise fertilizer application rates based on the soil conditions and wheat growth status. Besides, farmers typically purchase fertilizer in bulk and apply it directly to their fields. When farm size is relatively small, they tend to apply the entire amount purchased, which can result in over-application of fertilizer. In contrast, service providers who offer both FMS and fertilizer supply can optimize the use of fertilizer. By leveraging economies of scale, these providers can enhance the divisibility of fertilizer, allowing for more precise application rates that better match the needs of the land. This integration of services helps prevent overuse, promotes more efficient fertilizer management, and reduces the environmental impact of excessive fertilizer application.

Furthermore, an interesting finding is that the fertilizer reduction effect of outsourcing FMS from organizations that provide comprehensive services is higher relatively to that from individual providers. There are two possible explanations for this. One is organization providers of FMS, as the implementers and promoters of agricultural green development policies are more motivated to take the lead in demonstrating the benefits of using high-quality fertilizers or adopting fertilizers application services, while simultaneously establishing a strong reputation and brand to foster greater trust among farmers. The other explanation is that organization providers of FMS can use variable rate technology to accurately determine the timing, type, and quantity of fertilizer inputs needed. They are generally more proficient and professional in executing production tasks (Lu et al. 2022), contributing to reduced fertilizer application.

## 5.3 | Robustness Check—Inverse Probability Weighted Regression Adjustment (IPWRA)

To ascertain the reliability of the METE results, we utilized the doubly robust IPWRA estimation technique as a robustness check refer to following (Bello et al. 2024). The upper part of Table 4 presents the average treatment effect on the treated (ATT) estimated using the IPWRA. Outsourcing FMS from different service providers is significant and negatively associated with fertilizer application. The results indicate that outsourcing FMS decreases fertilizer applications of smallholder wheat farmers. We find that the estimates of the IPWRA are slightly different from the METE (i.e., insignificancy and differences in the magnitude of the estimated coefficients of some FMS combinations) model. This could be due to the inability of the IPWRA to control for unobserved heterogeneity. Some studies posited that the causal inference approaches (i.e., the marginal treatment effect, propensity score model) are different and might likely generate varied estimated results (Angrist and Krueger 1999; Zhou and Xie 2016). Nonetheless, both estimation techniques (METE and IPWRA) provide an interesting insight into the potential impact of outsourcing FMS on fertilizer application. Furthermore, we replaced the household agricultural labor variable with the number of household labor over the age of 60 to test the robustness of the model. The lower part of Table 4 reports the result. Outsourcing FMS (Ch1, Ch3, Ch4) significantly decreases fertilizer application. The findings are consistent with those in Table 4, implying that our results are robust.

## 5.4 | The Effects of Outsourcing FMS and Different FMS Providers on Wheat Yield

The previous analysis shows that outsourcing FMS contributes to reducing fertilizer application while contributing to production cost minimization. Here we focus on the impact of outsourcing FMS on wheat yields. We use the METE and OLS models to analyse the effect of outsourcing different FMS to the different service providers on wheat yield and the results are presented in Table 5 (The full results are attached Appendix Table A2). We find that outsourcing FMS to organization providers (Ch2 and Ch4) has a significant positive effect on yield. Farmers who outsourced FMS to organization providers had 0.06 percent and 0.05 percent more wheat yield compared to those who did not outsource. This suggests that organization providers have a higher welfare effect than individual providers. Especially, outsourcing both fertilizer application services and fertilizer supply to organizational providers can help farmers reduce fertilizer application and increase yields.

## 6 | Conclusions and Policy Implications

Smallholder farmers in many developing countries face multiple production constraints, including labor-shortage and small farm plots, which limit their scale of operation. The use of labor-saving technologies can substitute for scarce farm labor. Previous studies have shown that AMS plays an important role on improving farm performance. However, it is not known whether and how different FMS providers affect fertilizer application in China. This study bridges this knowledge gap by analyzing the effect of outsourcing FMS from individual and organizations service providers on fertilizer application and wheat yield.

Using the survey data collected from Shandong, Hebei, Henan, Shanxi Provinces of China, this study employed METE model to examine the determinants of the choices of FMS providers and its impact on fertilizer application and wheat yield. We checked the robustness of our results using the inverse probability weighted regression adjustment model. Our results show that gender, education level and risk attitude of the head of household, off-farm employment, farm size, plot topography and soil fertility, membership to cooperative and the availability of FMS at the village are related to farmers' likelihood of outsourcing FMS from different service providers. We find that outsourcing FMS from different service providers (Ch₁, Ch3, Ch4) reduces fertilizer applications by between 6.6 percent and 21.9 percent compared with non-outsourcing FMS. The reducing effect of outsourcing FMS and fertilizer supply to organization providers (Ch4) is greater than to individual providers. We also find that outsourcing FMS to organization providers increases wheat yields compared to outsourcing to individual providers. In other words, outsourcing FMS and fertilizer supply to organization providers have a higher welfare effect.

The findings of this study hold significant policy implications for advancing sustainable agricultural production and rural development. Our results reveal that farmers who apply fertilizer themselves tend to use larger quantities compared to those who outsource fertilizer application. This suggests that transitioning self-applying farmers to outsourcing could lead to a reduction in fertilizer use, thereby generating environmental, economic, and social benefits. Notably, only approximately 53% of households in our sample outsourced FMS, highlighting the urgent need for policies that promote the development and accessibility of FMS, especially for smallholder farmers with high fertilizer usage.

Moreover, outsourcing both FMS and fertilizer supply to organizational providers proves more effective in reducing fertilizer application and enhancing yields than relying on individual providers. This is primarily attributable to disparities in machinery quality and human capital. Organizational providers typically utilize more advanced machinery and possess greater technical expertise, whereas individual providers often face limitations in both asset quality and service proficiency. To address these challenges, policymakers should prioritize increasing subsidies to improve machinery quality, particularly for individual service providers, while implementing comprehensive training programs to enhance their technical skills and service standards. Encourage service organizations to strengthen communication with farmers on fertilization practices and actively promote the dissemination of scientifically sound fertilization knowledge. Additionally, farmers should be incentivized to adopt FMS. Governments and agricultural organizations should actively promote awareness of the economic and environmental advantages of outsourcing FMS and address adoption barriers through targeted outreach campaigns and the dissemination of success stories.

Our study has certain limitations. First, the analysis of the present study focuses solely on wheat farmers in four provinces of China, which may limit the generalizability of the findings. Given the regional diversity in crop types and economic conditions, further research on other crops, regions, and cross-country contexts is needed to provide a more comprehensive understanding of the welfare effects of outsourcing FMS. Second, despite efforts to address endogeneity, the cross-sectional nature of our data may not fully account for unobserved factors correlated with the instrumental variable. Future studies should employ panel data or experimental methods to strengthen causal identification.
