## Abstract

Purpose – The farm size-productivity relationship has long been the subject of debate among development economists. Few studies address this issue for China, and those that do only with outdated data sets poorly representing the current situation after the past decade of rapid change, which includes the rapid development of land rental markets, village labor out-migration and use of farm machines. Meanwhile, many studies have researched this relationship for Indian, which is undergoing similar changes except for the development of active land rental markets. The purpose of this paper is to measure the farm size-productivity relationship under the situations of rapid transformation in China and India. Design/methodology/approach – Based on the data of 325 Jiangxi and 400 Allahabad rice farmers in 2011, the survey covered multiple plots of each household in one/multiple growing season(s). The authors use the production function approach and the yield approach, and control for farmland quality, imperfect factor markets, and farm size measurement error, to identify the farm size-productivity relationship. Findings – The regressions show that land yields increase with plot size both by season and over the year in China. This may be one of the reasons that farm sizes are growing in some areas. In India, however, the inverse farm size-productivity relationship is observed by the study, despite recent changes. Moreover, land yields increase with farm machine use in both China and India. This result contributes to the debate over whether mechanization improves yields or just expands the land frontier. Originality/value – The paper empirically estimates the farm size-productivity relationship under rapid agrarian transformation in both China and India based on a unique data set collected by the authors in a detailed primary survey. The paper considers measurement error in the analysis, which adds values to this tvpe of analvsis.

The farm size-productivity relationship has long been the subject of debate among development economists. Economic theory shows that factor productivity should be equal across farms, and that land productivity and farm size should be uncorrelated (Feder, 1985). However, in empirical studies, an inverse farm size-productivity relationship has been widely observed in developing countries (Assuncao and Braido, 2007; Barrett et al., 2010; Carletto and Zezza, 2011). On the other hand, with the widespread usage of modern agricultural inputs, as well as labor saving modern machinery, it is believed that the inverse farm size-productivity relationship will gradually disappear (Foster and Rosenzweig, 2011). Studies of more developed countries, such as Brazil and Japan, have observed a positive farm size-productivity relationship (Helfand and Levine, 2004; Kawasaki, 2010).

For over five decades, the inverse farm-size productivity relationship has been discussed in Indian agriculture policy debates, and has constituted a core argument for the pursuit of both technological and institutional reforms to improve the efficiency and equity of the farm sector and in turn, rural growth and poverty reduction (as was the case after the Green Revolution in the 1970-early 1980s, when the objective of institutional reforms was to improve the income distribution from transactions in grain markets). The inverse farm size-productivity relationship in Indian agriculture is explained in terms of household-specific market imperfections (Carter, 1984; Feder, 1985); soil quality omission problems (Carter, 1984; Bhalla and Roy, 1988; Lamb, 2003); and farm size measurement error (Lamb, 2003). The inverse relationship may lead any policies that reduce landholding inequalities to have a positive effect on productivity; or reforms (such as the removal of discriminatory policies, or restrictions that limit or cause insecurity in land tenure contracts, and improved access to credit for small farmers) that enhance the flexibility of land markets to adjust the size distribution of farms and to lead to increases in efficiency, productivity, employment and equity, even in the absence of state-sponsored land redistribution.

Great changes have taken place in India, including the real farm wage's overall annual growth rate of 6.8 percent between 2007-2008 and 2011-2012 (Gulati et al., 2014). On one hand, according to Gulati et al. (2014), rural employment guarantee schemes distort rural labor markets by diverting rural labor to non-farm rural jobs and pushing up the average wage of casual workers and the cost of agricultural commodity production. On the other hand, the booming construction sector, which is growing at an average annual rate of 7.7 percent, along with the overall growth of the economy, act as pull factors for increased rural-urban migration and rising farm wages. Rising farm wages in turn accelerate mechanization, with 78 percent of small farms mechanized in 2006-2007 compared to 57 percent in the 1990s (Government of India, Respective issues). Agricultural production is now characterized by capital-led modern agricultural inputs. In total, 48 percent of the net sown area (of which, small farms constitute 50 percent) was irrigated in 2006-2007 compared to 37 percent (of which, small farms constitute 42 percent) in 1990-1991; overall fertilizer consumption per hectare increased from 76 kg in 1990-1991 to 147 kg in 2006-2007. However, the land rental market is still regulated, with 7.2, 8.5 and 6.5 percent of the total area operated being leased-in in 1981-1982, 1991-1992 and 2002-2003[1], respectively (National Sample Survey Organization (NSSO), 2002-2003). This may serve as a disincentive for the rational utilization of land, labor and capital.

Compared to related studies carried out in India, few studies have addressed the farm size-productivity relationship for China, and those that do, do so only through the use of outdated data sets that poorly represent the present situation after a decade of rapid change. According to the existing literature, the most recent data set is from Hubei Province from 1999 to 2003; Li et al. (2013) find land productivity to be inversely related with farm size. Moreover, following Benjamin (1995), Chen et al. (2011) use a panel data set covering nine provinces of China from 1995 to 1999 and find that the inverse relationship disappears after using IVs to control for land measurement error.

It must be noted that, China's agricultural production environment has changed dramatically since 2003. By the end of 2013, the number of non-farm workers reached 269 million, among which, 166 million were rural migrant workers (MOHRSS, 2014), constituting 16.5 percent of the total labor aged between 15 and 64 and 68.7 percent of agricultural labor (Rural Social and Economic Investigation Division, National Bureau of Statistics of China, 2014). Meanwhile, the agricultural sector has exhibited a net loss of productive labor. Average total hours spent on the farm per household has declined from 3,500 hours in 1991 to 2,000 hours in 2000, and further dropped to 1,400 hours in 2009 (de Brauw et al., 2012). At the same time, rural real daily wages doubled between 1998 and 2007 (Yu et al., 2012). In addition, increased demand for farm machinery from rising wages encourages smallholders to capture economies of scale through mechanization. From 1988 to 2011, the use of machines, chemical fertilizers and irrigation has increased 6.1, 3.9 and 1.3 percent annually[2], thereby leading the farm structure to undergo processes of capitalization and mechanization. Moreover, the land rental markets are now more vibrant, and by the end of June 2013, the use rights (land in China is owned by the state) of 20.67 million hectares of China's farmland, accounting for 23.9 percent of total farmland, have been transferred from its original owners to big farmers, cooperatives, etc. (Xinhuanet.com, 2013).

The changes taking place in the Chinese and Indian agricultural environments indicate that using decade-old data to study the farm size-productivity relationship will not be informative. In order to boost farmer productivity and strengthen food security, one of the new government strategies is the promotion of large farms and greater mechanization in China (NDRC, 2009). For India, policies related to the efficiency and equity of the farm sector should be informed by a strong evidence base. So, it is of great importance that the farm size-productivity relationship be re-examined in this current transitional period, as this will enhance policies designed to promote agricultural restructuring and development. In addition, studying China and India using a uniform analysis framework and questionnaire can help to distill meaningful conclusions. Third, According to Shangrao Economic and Social Yearbook (Shangrao Investigation Team, National Bureau of Statistics of China, 2012), total grain crop harvested area accounted for 74 percent of total crop harvested area in 2011, and cereal (including paddy, wheat and miscellaneous grains) output accounted for 97 percent of total grain output. Among them, paddy output accounted for 99.4 percent of cereal output. And data collected during the village census showed that paddy accounted for 64 percent of the total area harvested in kharif/monsoon in 2011 in India. So the paddy intensive farming mode in the surveyed places can eliminate bias by simply measuring rice yield, and avoiding the common problem of aggregating the data of different crops.

We assess the farm size-productivity relationship using a sample of 325 Chinese and 400 Indian rice farmers. This study constitutes the first on the current farm size-productivity relationship in China and India under rapid transformation. We find that land yields increase with plot size both by season and over the year using both the production function-based approach and the yield-based approach in China. This may be one of the reasons that farm sizes are growing in some areas. However, the inverse farm size-productivity relationship is observed in India using both methods, which corresponds with the findings of other related studies. Moreover, according to the production function method, land yields increase with farm machine use in both China and India. This result contributes to the debate over whether mechanization improves yields or just expands land frontiers. The results of this study are not only relevant for other southern provinces in China, but also for other developing countries and transitional economies with similar land policies and farming modes.

## 2. Sampling framework and data descriptive statistics

## 2.1 Sampling framework

The data used in this paper were collected in March and April of 2012 from rice farmers in Shangrao City of Jiangxi Province in China and Allahabad District of Eastern Uttar Pradesh (UP) in India. Jiangxi Province is the second largest indica-producing province in China. Shangrao City is the largest indica-producing city in the northeast of Jiangxi Province. Using the Shangrao Economic and Social Yearbook of 2011, Yanshan, Wannian and Yugan County were selected from the ten counties in Shangrao City according to grain-sown area, which ranks second, fifth and eighth, respectively. At the same time, the three counties better capture the different terrain characteristics, with Yugan County being located in the lake plains, Wannian County being located in the hilly area, and Yanshan County being partially locating in hilly area and partially in lake plains. Two townships per county and two villages per township were randomly selected. Using the county agricultural bureau's list of paddy farmers who received direct grain subsidies and comprehensive agricultural subsidies (which functions as a list of total paddy growing farmers within a village), 30 rice farmers were randomly selected per village and sorted. Given that Jiangxi experiences large out-migration, another 30 rice farmers per village were randomly selected and sorted as a substitute when someone from the first 30 selected migrated out. And of all the 360 substitute farmers, 133 were ultimately surveyed.

Eastern UP has come to the attention of the Government of India's “Bringing Green Revolution in Eastern India" program since 2009. Allahabad, as one of the main rice districts of eastern UP, produces 5 percent of the total paddy in the region. Information on the total rice area, yield, population and other key variables of all eight tehsils (an Indian administrative unit equivalent to a county) in Allahabad district was collected. The eight tehsils in the district were arranged in descending order and classified into three categories depending on the basis of their distance from the major consumption market named Jabalpur. Then one tehsil was chosen randomly from each of the nearest and farthest categories, and two were chosen randomly from those in the intermediate category. The choice of the tehsils captures the heterogeneity in terrain characteristics, agricultural activities and land use. Of the ten blocks in the four chosen tehsils, five blocks were randomly chosen. From each block, four villages were chosen randomly, making a total of 20 villages. In each selected village, a census of households was conducted, wherein each household was asked questions about their total land under cultivation and land under paddy cultivation. Of around 2,500 households in the census, 400 households were randomly selected and surveyed, including 20 households per village selected from an ordered listing of households by land size, from which ten households who cultivate more than 50 percent and another ten that cultivate 50 percent or less.

The household surveys focussed on recall of rice production activities in 2011. Information on the non-farm activities of household members and land rental were also collected. Two or three plots from each farm (Plots 1 and 2 indicate the largest two plots owned by a household, and Plot 3 indicates the largest rented or sharecropped plot, if any)

and total paddy land area in that season of the household is tracked. However, contrary to China, paddy is grown as a single season crop (kharif (July-September) season) in the Indian study zone. The unique feature of our data is that we collected detailed information on each stage of rice production (namely, seedling, land preparation, transplanting, weeding, fertilizing, pest control, threshing, harvesting, irrigation and drying) and also for each season where applicable (or for each indica growing season (i.e. early indica, middle indica and late indica), in the case of China). We also carried out supplementary surveys of village officials to collect the main agricultural economic statistics. The final sample contains 325 households from 12 villages for China and 400 households from 20 villages for India. The plot-level data structure for the samples is shown in Table I.

## 2.2 Variable definitions and descriptive statistics

Based on the existing literature (Barrett et al., 2010; Chen et al., 2011; Ali and Deininger 2014), the variable categories we used empirically are production inputs, household characteristics, paddy characteristics, and soil and land controls. Production inputs include land in ha, family labor input in day/ha, hired labor input in day/ha, agricultural machinery service, seed, fertilizer, pesticide, herbicide and irrigation, all of the rest are measured in USD/ha. To check whether out-migration will affect paddy yield, we define household members as people under the same registered residence and who have spent a combined duration of no less than six months in the same household in the past 12 months. Children are defined as younger than 16 in China and 15 in India, and elderly as above 65 years old in both countries. Female labor is defined as non-student, non-soldier and able-bodied female between 16 to 64 years of age in China, and 15 to 65 years in India. Children and the elderly are considered to be dependents in India. Other household characteristics include age and education of household head, member dummy of Communist Party of China or Panchayat (a local self-government organization) dummy for comparability, agricultural scientific-technological demonstration household dummy or access to agricultural extension services dummy for comparability, tractor ownership dummy, cattle ownership dummy and total cultivated paddy land area. Paddy characteristics include paddy growing season dummy, paddy quality dummy (both not applicable for India) and hybrid seed dummy. Soil and land controls contain plot dummy, land fertility level, irrigation convenience level, total number of fallowing months of the year, plot location (i.e. terrain dummy, plot group dummy and high-yield demonstration area dummy) and land rent.

Rice farmers mostly operate on a small scale, in general. According to the Shangrao Economic and Social Statistical Yearbook of 2011, per capita paddy area in Yanshan, Wannian and Yugan County, is 0.71, 0.86 and 0.79 mu[3], respectively. Per capita dry land area from the village survey is 0.11, 0.17 and 0.05 mu, respectively. As the agricultural tax has been abolished since January 1, 2006 in China (after which no taxes were collected based on operational area), and agricultural income share continues to decrease thanks to off-farm employment, with land no longer being regarded as a proxy for farmers' wealth. So the authors assume that farmers have no incentive to over-report or under-report actual operational scale. Given that non-standard mu might be used, we added relevant questions such as “Is the reported area measured in standard mu? If not, what is the real size of your operational scale after converting into standard mu?" to the household questionnaire. According to each farmer's reply, we adjusted accordingly to obtain the plot area and operational area, while the latter is the manifestation of the total farm scale. With very small plots such as those encountered in our survey in China, GPS measurements may result in greater measurement error (Carletto and Zezza, 2011), thus the adjusted area variables we use for China is a better approximation. In India, the per capita paddy area is 0.22, 0.196, 0.142 and 0.252 ha for the Bara, Koraon, Soraon and Phulpur tehsils, respectively. On average, 67 percent of the agricultural area is irrigated. This proportion varies from 50 percent in Bara to as high as 85 percent for the Soraon tehsil. The average size of per capita irrigated farmland is 0.39 ha.

Tables II and III represent the mean descriptive statistics for rice farmers at the plot level. In order to assess the influence of multiple cropping on the farm size-land productivity relationship, two measurements are used for farm size and production inputs variables, both of which are checked empirically. Measurement 1 indicates plot area and does not consider multiple cropping. Paddy production inputs per hectare and yields are obtained by dividing these variables by plot area. This measurement is used for India and China. While for China, another measurement is used simultaneously to capture the seasonality in rice production. Measurement 2 indicates harvested area of the plot, which is the aggregate of plot area in each growing season. Paddy production inputs per hectare and yields are obtained by dividing harvested area of the plot. The multiple cropping index in China indicates the paddy land use maximization rate, and is obtained by dividing the actual harvested area by the total paddy area. Farming more seasons a year means the farmer is more specialized. Farmers in Jiangxi can grow late indica on the same field after early indica is harvested, or opt to grow only middle indica for the year.

For plot rent, only plot rental/sharecropping information for Plot 3 is collected for China. First, we adjusted sharecropping into monetary rent based on the output of the plot, the percentage of sharecropping, and the average sales price of paddy in that season. We then ran plot rent determinants estimation regressions based on the information of Plot 3[4]. Plot rent is mainly determined by the village dummy. Within a village, plot-to-home distance and the paddy demonstration area dummy both determine plot rent. Using the coefficients we estimated, we obtained the shadow land rent for Plots 1 and 2. The share of rental area in total arable land is very low in the survey location in India. We supplement this with information about the annual rentals for the rented-in plots collected in our village surveys. Given that our study location has two major crop cycles and paddy is cultivated only as a kharif crop, we divided the average annual rental by two. Then we calculated the rentals for the owned land. In the existing literature, land rent is either not considered, or obtained through village specialists who are asked about the potential sale value of different topographies and locations (Assuncao and Braido, 2007), so we believe our methods of deriving plot rent are effective.

## 2.3 General discussion of differences in the rice farming environments in China and India

Based on the final report using the same data set as this paper of the survey in both China and India (Chen et al., 2013), several salient points emerge.

First, operational farm size is growing more quickly in China than in India. Operational farm size in China (which essentially refers to rice farm size, as these farms are very specialized in rice) jumps from 0.9 ha in 2007 to 1.5 ha in 2011. However, in India, the average operational farm size rises slightly, from 0.73 to 0.83 ha over the five years recalled.

Second, the rural factor market is more developed in China than it is in India, while the land rental market is far more active in China. The jump in operational farm size in

In addition, the seed market is somewhat limited in India. There is variation in the commercialization of the seed sector, which correlates with the share of hybrids or other certified seeds, such as 22 percent buying seed in India vs 81-93 percent buying seed in China.

Third, surveyed Chinese rice farmers own more farm machines compared to those in India. An extreme correlation between land size and tractor ownership is found in China, and on average 27 percent of rice farmers own tractors. But there is widespread use of tractors via tractor service renting, as 79 percent of rice farmers used tractors in 2011, an increase from 68 percent in 2007. Very few farmers (3 percent) own harvesters. But nearly all farmers (83 percent) use mechanical harvesters, increasing from 68 percent in 2007, and corresponding with the growing trend of large harvester operation outsourcing as illustrated by Yang et al. (2013). In India, only 10 percent of farmers own tractors, and even fewer farmers (2 percent) own threshers. However, 88 percent of farms hire tractors to prepare the land. By contrast, unlike in China, machine harvesting is not done in India; it is all done by hand. But some 60 percent of farmers hire threshing machines.

As machines are substituted for labor, the uptake of machine services appears to be correlated with the rise of the rural wage rate, which in turn is correlated with rising rural non-farm employment and migration, which tightens rural labor markets (Reardon et al., 2014). Over the entire period of 1990-1991 to 2011-2012, real farm wages grew at an average annual growth rate of 2.9 percent in India (Gulati et al., 2014). Foster and Rosenzweig (2004) note that in India, during the period of 1971-1982, there was a significant increase in the share of total non-farm income in total income, from 19.3 percent to almost a third. And that share rose to 48.1 percent by 1999. All of the increase in non-farm incomes was in the form of wages and salary. While in China, between 1991 and 2006, real wages grew continuously at an annual rate of 10.2 and 9.3 percent in urban and rural sites, respectively. In 1998-2008, rural per capita real income growth accelerated in both sectors, reaching 5.9 and 9.1 percent in rural and urban areas, respectively (Ge and Yang, 2011).

Fourth, more surveyed rice farms participate in non-farm work in China than those do in India. In China, three-quarters of the farm households feature off-farm employment, and about half of the farms undertake migration, corresponding with the findings by Deininger et al. (2014) that some 15 percent of sample households exited agriculture and that the mean share of household labor supplied to the non-agricultural sector increased from less than 50 percent to more than 60 percent during 2000-2008. However, in India, only a quarter of the farm households are undertaking off-farm employment. Migration was also limited, at about 13 percent in 2011, and took place more often within UP than beyond it. Indeed, Mukherjee and Zhang (2007) note that the growth of rural non-farm employment in China was more rapid than in India, especially over the decade of the 1990s. By 2000, China's rural non-farm employment doubled that of India's.

Herbicide is often a substitute for labor. Less farmers use herbicide in India than do those in China, as 99 percent of rice farmers does so in China vs only 11 percent in India. We read that difference as being due to the greater pressure on wages from rural non-farm and migration employment in China compared within India.

## 4. Estimation results

Following the empirical framework, we adopted two distinct estimation approaches namely, the Cobb-Douglas (C-D) production function approach and the yield-based approach, to estimate the farm size-productivity relationship (see Assuncao and Braido, 2007; Barrett et al., 2010). Taylor and Scott Shonkwiler (1986) pointed out that the C-D production function adequately represents the general production technology. For some observations, input values equal zero. Following Macurdy and Pencavel (1986), we added 0.001 to all observations for all inputs so as not to introduce bias by dropping observations in a non-random fashion. We used STATA 11 software packages and the estimation method referred to by Cameron and Trivedi (2005). We estimated the farm size-productivity relationship on the plot level. For plot-level analysis, two time dimensions are examined for China, that include plot-level seasonal data and plot-level yearly data; while for India we consider only the latter, as only one season is grown.

Our results do not support clear paddy quality-yield relationship. A possible explanation might be that farmers may not have a uniform interpretation of paddy quality. Or farmers may have a poor understanding of paddy quality and seed features, as both are self-reported in the survey.

## 4.2 Plot-level yearly estimation results for China[6]

Summing the seasonal output and agricultural inputs up, we analyzed the relationship between land productivity and farm size on plot level by year, based on different aforementioned definitions of farm size. The results show that the correlation coefficient between yield and plot area equals to 0.09 logarithmically, which is significant, but insignificant at -0.02 when computed in levels under Measurement 1. Under Measurement 2, this statistic is positive and statistically significant at the 1 percent level, equaling 0.28 when computed in logarithms and 0.17 in levels.

Table V presents the plot-level yearly estimation results of the C-D production function approach under Measurement 1 for China. All the variables are the same as in Table IV except for the use of the multiple cropping index as paddy characteristics. The coefficients of Models 3-9 in Table V are highly similar to the corresponding coefficients of corresponding models in Table IV. This means that, after controlling for other influences, the elasticity of yield to plot area is stable. The multiple cropping index variable is positive and statistically significant at the 1 percent level. The effect of the total cultivated paddy land area variable on yield is insignificant in Model 7, which is consistent with the findings of Assuncao and Braido (2007) and Barrett et al. (2010). The agricultural machinery service cost variable is positive and the coefficients (Models 3-7) are comparable with the corresponding coefficients in Table IV. Moreover, in the household fixed effects model (Model 9), this variable is still positive and statistically significant at the 5 percent level. In the pooled cross-section model (Model 6), the higher the children in household ratio is, the lower the paddy yield. However, this effect disappears in the village fixed effects model. The household elder ratio and female labor ratio variables do not affect paddy yield, suggesting that out-migration of male labor from Jiangxi villages do not decrease paddy yield. With other influences controlled for, higher education of household heads and scientific-technological demonstration household status increase paddy yields. Similar to the findings in Table IV, in the household fixed effects model, land plot source highly influences yield. Compared to the yield of Plot 3, Plot 1 has higher yield, while Plot 2 has lower yield.

## 4.3 Plot-level yearly estimation results for India[7]

The empirical analysis for India confirms the inverse farm size-productivity relationship, as increasing plot area by 1 percent results in an average 0.015 percent decrease in yield (Model 8). Productivity is significantly positively impacted by input intensification, such as irrigation and agricultural machinery service cost (Model 8). This provides a rationale for agricultural mechanization and simultaneously can explain the positive relationship between tractor ownership and productivity (Model 5). No statistically significant relationship is found between labor use, other inputs and productivity, which are the same as the situation found in China.

Regarding the household characteristics, an inverse relationship is found with the dependency ratio in Model 5; however, this effect disappears once controlling for village-level unobserved factors. Though the coefficient of the female labor ratio variable is negative, it is not statistically significant; indicating labor structure within the household has no clear influence on yield. Similarly as in China, education level has a positive impact on productivity; but access to extension service barely has any significant impact on productivity, which is perhaps indicative of the poor status of the current service delivery system in India. Plot characteristics barely have any significant relationship with yield (Model 8) (Table VI).

We argue that the agrarian transformations taking place in China have converted this previously inverse relationship into a direct relationship, but this reversal has not yet taken place in India. Using a detailed primary survey of a sample of 325 Jiangxi and 400 Allahabad rice farmers in 2011, which covered multiple plots in each household in one/multiple planting season(s), the authors were able to pair the usual household- and plot-level variables used in productivity analyses with soil and land controls. This paper has tested the farm size-productivity relationship under rapid agrarian transformations in both China and India, using both the production function-based approach and the yield-based approach. Key findings are as follows.

A strong positive plot size-productivity relationship exists both seasonally and yearly by both measurements in China, taking multiple cropping into consideration This is significantly different from the inverse farm size-productivity relationship frequently observed in the existing literature. Our results can be seen in light of the development of Chinese agriculture under rapid transformation and as after Chen et al. (2011), who find that the inverse relationship disappears after using IV methods to control for land area measurement error using a data set of nine provinces of China from 1995 to 1999. With the further development of the land rental market, village labor out-migration and mechanization in Chinese agriculture, our results show that important changes have taken place, which might justify efforts to encourage famers to expand their operational scales, and also to demonstrate that it is in line with development goals to enforce a high-quality farmland construction policy in China. A high-quality farmland construction policy mainly aims to enlarge the operational plot area, by upgrading land infrastructure, such as roads, irrigation facilities, etc. So, in appropriate plain areas, a high-quality farmland construction policy should be supported and well executed. In India, however, the situation has been found to correspond with the prevailing paradigm in the literature, and the inverse farm size-productivity relationship has been confirmed by our study despite recent changes.

Moreover, land yields increase with farm machine use in both China and India. This sheds new light on the debate over whether mechanization improves yields or simply expands land frontiers. Thus reforming the policy and regulatory framework governing land lease and rental markets in India emerges as a high priority to sustain productivity. In China, determining how to deepen land system reform and build high-quality farmland, while keeping the reasonable consolidation and scientific layout of farmland in mind, is important for creating a favorable infrastructural environment for the operation of agricultural machinery. Machinery purchase subsidization is one good way to increase rice farmers' demand for machinery, which has been widely executed in China and which has achieved satisfactory results. Meanwhile, as rice farmers increasingly rely on outsourcing services provided by specialized farmers' cooperatives, big family farm owners, etc., we should accelerate the construction of public service organizations, and actively support the development of professional agricultural cooperatives, which are not only capable of naturally reducing machinery service prices, but which are also able to enjoy scale effects in certain rice growing stages, such as harvesting. India can learn from China's experiences with mechanization policies.

With the out-migration of a large number of young rural laborers, providing left-behind farmers with training and education through agricultural extension activities will significantly promote land productivity. In this context, promoting a pluralistic agricultural extension services system with an improved focus on community outreach, reinforced organizational autonomy, improved staff quality and a service delivery mechanism is a top priority.
