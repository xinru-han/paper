# Land transfer-in choices, agricultural mechanization services, and farm performance: insights from Chinese grain farmers

Pengcheng Wang and Kaiyuan Huang D

School of Economics, Guizhou University, Guiyang, China

## ABSTRACT

This study examines the impact of land transfer-in choices on grain farmers' adoption of agricultural mechanization services (AMS), as well as the heterogeneous effects of both factors on the distribution of farm performance (measured by grain yields and net returns), using publicly available data from the 2020 China Rural Revitalization Survey. To address potential selection bias and capture distributional changes, we employ a multivalued treatment effects (MVTE) model and an unconditional quantile regression (UQR) model. Unlike previous studies that consider only whether land is transferred in or out, we distinguish among three types of land transfer-in choices: no land transfer-in, fragmented land transfer-in, and contiguous land transfer-in. The empirical results show that relative to no land transfer-in, both fragmented and contiguous transfer-in significantly promote AMS adoption, with the effect being stronger for contiguous transfer-in. In addition, both contiguous (as opposed to fragmented) land transfer-in and AMS adoption have significant positive effects on grain yields and net returns, with these effects being more pronounced at higher performance quantiles. These findings highlight the complementary roles of contiguous land transfer-in and AMS adoption in advancing agricultural modernization, offering policy insights for enhancing smallholder productivity and promoting the sustainable transformation of Chinese agriculture.

## I. Introduction

In many countries across Asia, Africa, and Latin America, smallholder farmers form the foundation of agricultural production, holding a substantial share of land and labour resources. In China, for example, the Third National Agricultural Census reports that smallholders comprise approximately 98% of all agricultural operators and manage about 70% of the nation's cultivated land (X. Chen et al. 2022). However, the self-subsistence production model prevalent among smallholders is often associated with low land productivity and high opportunity costs (Abdul-Rahaman, Issahaku, and Zereyesus 2021; Oumer et al. 2020; Sklenicka 2016; Takeshima 2018). Meanwhile, rapid urbanization has intensified rural labour shortages (Zou and Mishra 2024), further undermining farm performance. To address these challenges, a growing body of research shows that agricultural mechanization can help mitigate rural labour constraints and enhance total factor productivity (Guo et al. 2022; Zhou and Ma 2022). Nevertheless, the adoption of mechanization among smallholders in developing countries remains limited, largely due to the mismatch between farm size and the economies of scale required for efficient machinery use (Tufa et al. 2024). In contrast, evidence from developed countries such as the United States, Japan, and Israel suggests that promoting agricultural mechanization services (AMS) can facilitate mechanization across production stages and expand the share of farm operations conducted with machinery.

A review of the literature reveals that AMS has contributed significantly to various dimensions of farm economic, production, and environmental performance, including rural income (Baiyegunhi, Majokweni, and Ferrer 2019; Zheng, Ma, and Zhou 2021), farm efficiency (Huan, Dong, and Chi 2022; X. Ma et al. 2024), agricultural productivity (Deng et al. 2020; Lu, Du, and Qiu 2022; Zou and Mishra 2024), and sustainable development (Du et al. 2024). For instance, Baiyegunhi, Majokweni, and Ferrer (2019), based on a survey of 300 households in a South African village, found that the adoption of AMS increased net crop income by 2700 ZAR.

Huan, Dong, and Chi (2022) demonstrated that AMS influences the allocation of production factors, thereby enhancing farm efficiency. Similarly, Zou and Mishra (2024) argued that AMS improves agricultural productivity and helps increase household income by enhancing production efficiency and facilitating off-farm employment. In addition, Du et al. (2024), drawing on data from 1040 farmers in the Yellow River region of China, found that the promotion of AMS enhances the likelihood of adopting low-carbon technologies, thus promoting sustainable agricultural development.

Although AMS has proven effective in supporting smallholder management in some developed countries, the AMS markets in most developing countries remain underdeveloped and face significant challenges. Taking China as a case in point, small and fragmented land endowments constitute the most salient production characteristic of smallholders (M. Chen et al. 2022; X. Zhang, Yang, and Thomas 2017). Such factor endowments result in high transaction costs when outsourcing production activities such as tillage, crop protection, and harvesting to service providers. Consequently, AMS providers tend to favour serving larger farms with contiguous land distributions (Qiu, Shi, et al. 2021). Furthermore, as large-scale farms increasingly rely on AMS for production, they are highly likely to expand production by consolidating smallholders land in order to appropriate a larger share of the benefits generated by economies of scale, which may in turn result in the crowding out of smallholders from the agricultural sector (Y. Chen and Jiao 2024; Qiu, Choy, et al. 2021; Zhao, Wu, and Qiu 2025), which points to a structural imbalance on the supply side of China's AMS market. Even more concerning is that the small-scale and fragmented nature of smallholders remains the primary factor underlying their insufficient effective demand for AMS. On the one hand, limited farm size implies that the scarce labour resources of smallholders are generally sufficient to meet production needs. Although AMS can enhance household production efficiency, the longstanding self-subsistence production model constrains smallholders' awareness of improving efficiency through indirect investment (Zheng, Ma, and Zhou 2021). On the other hand, the fragmented nature of farmland inevitably raises the service costs of AMS, which are typically passed on to smallholders, thereby dampening their willingness to adopt such services (Qiu, Choy, et al. 2021). Consequently, overcoming the adoption dilemma arising from smallholders' resource constraints has become a critical challenge for policymakers.

For reasons of fairness, China implemented socialist agricultural reforms between 1953 and 1956, during which land was designated as collectively owned and most of it was evenly distributed among farmers. However, due to unclear property rights, farmers were unable to freely transfer land, and this land tenure system contributed to persistent land fragmentation in China for many years. After 1978, the Household Responsibility System was introduced, under which land remained collectively owned but farmers were allowed to freely manage and produce on it. Unlike some developed countries in Europe and North America, where farm owners possess both land ownership and transfer rights, Chinese farmers only hold contracting rights and transferable management rights; that is, they can transfer land only through leasing rather than direct sale. Consequently, at the early stage of this system, the boundaries of contracted land were often unclear. Lessors, concerned that leased plots might be adjacent to their existing plots and therefore partially annexed during the leasing process, mostly engaged in fragmented land transfer-in rather than contiguous land transfer-in (Xu, Chen, and Ahmed 2024). Specifically, fragmented land transfer-in refers to the situation where the leased plot is separate from the lessee's existing plots, whereas contiguous land transfer-in refers to leased plots that are adjacent to existing plots. Evidently, due to the immobile physical nature of land, fragmented land transfer-in only helps expand the overall operational scale but does not alleviate the constraints associated with small and fragmented plots at the local level (B. Li and Shen 2021; Y. Li et al. 2024). This not only hampers mechanized production but may also increase the cost of AMS as plot fragmentation intensifies. To address these issues, the Chinese government further implemented the policy of separating ownership, contracting, and management rights, and clarified plot boundaries through land registration, thereby removing barriers to contiguous land transfer-in.

A review of the existing literature (Lu, Du, and Qiu 2022; Qian et al. 2022; Tufa et al. 2024; Xu,

Chen, and Ahmed 2024; Zheng, Ma, and Zhou 2021) indicates that the mechanisms through which land transfer-in affects AMS adoption mainly operate through three channels: promoting farm scale enlargement, reducing transaction costs, and encouraging investment. First, whether fragmented or contiguous, land transfer-in enables the consolidation of scattered small plots into the hands of certain operators, thereby achieving an appropriate scale of farming. This reduces the marginal cost of mechanized production and consequently enhances farmers' willingness to adopt AMS (Tufa et al. 2024). Second, compared with fragmented transfer-in, contiguous land transferin can avoid the relocation losses of machinery between scattered plots and provide a more suitable environment for the efficient operation of large machinery (Y. Li et al. 2024; Qiu, Choy, et al. 2021). This helps lower service transaction costs and improve machinery utilization, making AMS providers more willing to serve such groups (Qiu, Shi, et al. 2021). Third, given the same farm scale, financial institutions such as banks are more inclined to extend credit to farmers with higher land contiguity (Motsoari, Cloete, and Van Schalkwyk 2015). Furthermore, a higher degree of land contiguity increases the likelihood that farmers will sign long-term contracts with AMS providers and adopt full-process agricultural production outsourcing services (Zheng, Ma, and Zhou 2021). In summary, although fragmented land transfer-in may expand farm size, the dispersed nature of such plots tends to weaken the resulting economies of scale. By contrast, contiguous land transfer-in is more effective in alleviating the tension between smallholders' resource endowments and the scale economies required by AMS adoption.

As discussed earlier, the subsistence-oriented production mode of smallholders is closely tied to low land productivity, largely due to highly fragmented land endowments and the low efficiency of labour-intensive production. By contrast, contiguous land transfer-in directly improves factor allocation among farmers, while the adoption of AMS enables mechanized production at relatively low input costs, thereby enhancing agricultural total factor productivity. What has been largely overlooked in the existing literature, however, is the potential synergy between contiguous land transfer-in and AMS adoption in shaping farm performance. On the one hand, contiguous land transfer-in alleviates land fragmentation by consolidating adjacent plots, which not only reduces boundary-related inefficiencies and the risk of land abandonment (Wang et al. 2022), but also mitigates the efficiency losses and cost increases associated with AMS operations, thus improving farm performance from the cost side (Lu, Du, and Qiu 2022; Qiu, Choy, et al. 2021). In addition, although contiguous land transfer-in expands farm scale, it may exacerbate labour shortages caused by rural depopulation and ageing (Yamauchi 2016). AMS, in turn, helps relax farmers' financial and labour constraints, enabling mechanization through indirect investment, which enhances both the precision and scalability of agricultural production, thereby improving farm performance from the revenue side (Yin et al. 2024; Zhao, Wu, and Qiu 2025). Hence, incorporating AMS adoption into the analytical framework is essential when examining the effect of contiguous land transfer-in on farm performance.

The main contributions of this study can be summarized in three aspects. First, unlike previous literature that largely overlooks the types of land transferred in, this study delves into the factors affecting land transfer-in choices (i.e. no land transfer-in, contiguous and fragmented land transfer-in), and the differential impacts of land transfer-in choices on AMS adoption, thereby enriching the literature on land transfer and AMS. Second, to facilitate a more intuitive comparison of the differential impacts of contiguous land transfer-in and fragmented land transfer-in on AMS adoption, we employ the multivalued treatment effects (MVTE) model for empirical analysis, which was rarely used in related studies. Furthermore, we further analyse the heterogeneity of these differential impacts between smallholder farmers and large-scale farms. Third, from the dual perspectives of productivity and production efficiency, this article further examines the heterogeneous effects of land transfer-in choices and AMS adoption on the distribution of farm performance. By employing an unconditional quantile regression (UQR) model for estimation, the study extends the analytical frontier of research on farm performance.

The remainder of the article is organized as follows: The next section outlines the empirical strategies. Section III presents the data, key variable measurements, and descriptive statistics. Section IV reports and discusses the empirical results. The final section concludes and offers policy implications.

## II. Estimation strategies

## Multivalued treatment effects (MVTE) model

The decisions of farmers to engage in contiguous land transfer-in or fragmented land transfer-in are related to household head, household, and even village-level characteristics, which may give rise to sample selection bias. When multiple choices are present, existing studies mainly employ the multivalued treatment effects (MVTE) model or the multinomial endogenous switching regression (MESR) model to address this issue and obtain unbiased estimates (Linden et al. 2016; W. Ma, Zhu, and Zhou 2022; Takeshima 2018; Zhou and Ma 2022). However, the unbiased estimation in the MESR framework critically depends on the availability of valid instrumental variables. Since no suitable instrumental variables could be identified from the publicly accessible database, we adopt the MVTE model to assess the impact of land transfer-in choices on AMS adoption.

The estimation procedure of the MVTE model consists of two stages. The first stage identifies the determinants of farmers' land transfer-in choices – that is, which household head, household, and village-level characteristics influence the decision to choose among no land transfer-in', fragmented land transfer-in', and contiguous land transferin'. The conclusions from this stage provide useful implications for policymakers seeking to guide farmers' land transfer-in choices. Following previous studies (Takeshima 2018; Zhou and Ma 2022), we employ a multinomial logit (MNL) model to evaluate how the selected explanatory variables affect farmers' land transfer-in choices. ## Unconditional quantile regression (UQR) model

While previous studies have explored the respective impacts of either land transfer-in or AMS adoption on farm performance (Y. Li et al. 2024; Lu, Du, and Qiu 2022; X. Ma et al. 2024; Zheng, Ma, and Zhou 2021), our focus is distinct. We are primarily interested in the joint impact of land transfer-in choices and AMS adoption, as well as how their effects vary across the distribution of farm performance. To this end, we employ the UQR model to capture the heterogeneous impacts of these two factors. Compared to the conventional conditional quantile regression model, the UQR model is less sensitive to the choice of control variables and reflects changes in the overall distribution of farm performance rather than conditional distributions given specific control variables (Chang and Mishra 2012; Firpo, Fortin, and Lemieux 2009; W. Ma and Wang 2020). Therefore, the UQR model is more appropriate for our analysis.

## III. Data, key variable measurements, and descriptive statistics

## Data

The data used in this study are drawn from the 2020 China Rural Revitalization Survey (CRRS) conducted by the Rural Development Institute of the Chinese Academy of Social Sciences. The survey employed a stratified random sampling approach, selecting one-third of the provinces from the eastern, central, western, and northeastern regions of China. It covered 50 counties, 150 townships, 300 villages, over 3800 households, and approximately 15,000 household members, taking into account regional variations in economic development, geographic location, and agricultural conditions. Sampling at the county, township, and village levels was primarily based on per capita GDP rankings, followed by systematic random sampling. Within each village, households residing at home were randomly divided into 14 groups, and one household from each group (with two alternates) was selected via systematic random sampling, ensuring both representativeness and methodological rigour. The data processing in this study involved three main steps. First, household-level and village-level data were merged. Second, households not engaged in grain crop production were excluded. Finally, after removing missing and outlier values for the selected variables, the final sample consisted of 2024 grain farmers.

## Key variable measurements

## AMS adoption

Unlike the broader notions of agricultural socialized services and outsourcing services discussed in the existing literature, agricultural mechanization services (AMS) is a more narrowly defined concept. Specifically, it captures farmers' adoption of AMS in six stages of agricultural production: (1) plowing, (2) sowing, (3) pesticide application, (4) fertilization, (5) irrigation, and (6) harvesting and transportation. Because simply counting the number of AMS stages adopted may not capture heterogeneity across production stages, we follow prior studies (Zheng, Ma, and Zhou 2021) and use AMS adoption costs per mu in 2019 as a proxy variable for AMS adoption. The unit of measurement is 1000 yuan/mu (1 mu = 1/15 hectare (≈0.0667 ha); 1 USD = 6.91 yuan in 2019).

## Land transfer-in choices

In this study, the treatment variable, land transferin choices, is treated as a multinomial variable with three possible outcomes. If a household has no land transfer-in, the treatment variable is assigned a value of 1. Similarly, if the household transferred in only fragmented land, the value is 2; if it transferred in only contiguous land, the value is 3. Thus, the land transfer-in choices take integer values between 1 and 3. This specification is consistent with the requirements of the MVTE model for handling such variables.

## Farm performance

Farm performance primarily refers to the farm's economic performance, measured by grain yields and net returns, specifically total yields and net returns in 2019, expressed in units of 1000 kg and 1000 yuan, respectively. Net returns are defined as the difference between total grain income and production costs. Production costs mainly consist of factor input expenditures during the production process, including seeds and seedlings, fertilizers, pesticides, irrigation, machinery, and hired labour. It should be noted that grain yields and net returns are not measured at the mean level, because this study focuses on how land transfer-in choices and AMS adoption affect farm performance across different farm sizes, and whether these effects help narrow the performance gap between smallholder farmers and large-scale farms. However, identifying quantiles of per mu grain yields and net returns based on the UQR model is unlikely to capture the scale characteristics of farms. Therefore, this study mainly adopts total grain yields and net returns perspective for measurement, while the estimates of per mu grain yields and net returns are used for reference.

## IV. Empirical results

## Factors affecting land transfer-in choices

The first objective of this article is to examine the factors associated with farmers' land transfer-in choices. Table 3 presents the estimation results of the MNL model; for ease of interpretation, we report only the marginal effects. The results indicate that for each additional unit of family size and farm size, the probability of a farmer transferring in fragmented land increases by 2.7% and 0.5%, respectively, while the probability of contiguous land transfer-in increases by 1% and 0.2%, respectively. This suggests that whether farmers transfer in land is closely related to their labour and land input factors, which is supported by Qiu and Luo (2021).

However, we note that the age of the household head, dependency ratio, assets ownership, the number of plots, and village per capita cultivated are all significantly associated with whether farmers transfer in fragmented land, and the associations are statistically significant. However, these factors are not significantly related to contiguous land transfer-in. This result may stem from the structural imbalance in the supply side of China's land transfer market. In China, farmers typically only transfer out land when labour is extremely scarce, which limits the options for those willing to transfer in contiguous land (C. Zhang, Nie, and Zhu 2024). Moreover, most land transfers occur among acquaintances. Even when contiguous land is available, the transferor often prefers to transfer out land to farmers without adjacent plots to avoid property disputes and protect social relationships (Xu, Chen, and Ahmed 2024). Therefore, whether farmers can transfer-in contiguous land may depend more on supply-side structural reforms than on optimizing demand-side endowment.

## Average treatment effects of land transfer-in choices

The second objective of this article is to examine the impact of land transfer-in choices on grain farmers' adoption of AMS. Table 4 reports the ATE of different land transfer-in choices on AMS adoption, along with their corresponding percentage changes for ease of interpretation. The estimation results of the MVTE model using the IPWRA estimator indicate that, compared with farmers who did not transfer in any land, those who transferred in fragmented land increased their AMS adoption expenditure per mu by 47.4%, while those who transferred in contiguous land increased it by 63.8%. This suggests that contiguous land transfer-in is more conducive to promoting AMS adoption than fragmented land transfer-in. As discussed in Section I, although fragmented land transfer-in helps expand the operational scale, it fails to reduce the transaction costs of AMS adoption or improve access to credit. In other words, it does not eliminate discrimination from AMS suppliers and financial institutions, which undermines the scale-economy effects of land transfer-in and weakens farmers' willingness to adopt AMS. By comparison, contiguous land transfer-in exhibits stronger economies of scale, which not only helps alleviate structural supply-demand imbalances but also encourages farmers to increase productive investments through improved farm performance, thereby promoting greater adoption of AMS.

## Disaggregated analysis by farm size

Meanwhile, the heterogeneous impact of land transfer-in choices on AMS adoption across farms of varying scales is another key focus of this study, as it helps identify which types of land transfer-in encourage greater AMS adoption among smallholder farmers. Following the FAO's commonly used classification standard, we define smallholders as those operating farms smaller than 30 mu (2 hectares), while large-scale farms exceed this threshold. Using the MVTE model based on the IPWRA estimator, we conducted disaggregated analysis by farm size, with results presented in Table 5. The findings indicate that, for smallholders without any land transfer-in, acquiring fragmented land transfer-in increases their AMS expenditure per mu by 82.3%, while acquiring contiguous land transfer-in raises it by 77.7%. This suggests that, compared to contiguous land transfer-in, smallholders transferred in fragmented land are more likely to adopt additional AMS.

This result can be understood in light of Zheng, Ma, and Zhou (2021), who note that contiguous land distribution helps smallholders save time and coordination costs associated with managing dispersed plots and improves the utilization rate of self-owned agricultural machinery. In contrast, fragmented land distribution exacerbates labour shortages, increases farm management complexity (Sklenicka 2016; Wang et al. 2022), and raises the marginal cost of using self-owned agricultural machinery. These effects are particularly pronounced for smallholders without economies of scale, thereby strengthening their incentive to adopt AMS. For large-scale farms, the ATE of either fragmented or contiguous land transfer-in is both positive relative to no land transfer-in, with the ATE for contiguous land transfer-in being higher than that for fragmented land transfer-in. However, neither estimate is statistically significant. This result partly supports the earlier argument: when farms exhibit economies of scale, their lower production costs per mu and stronger self-management capacity often lead them to prefer self-owned agricultural machinery over adopting AMS (Qian et al. 2022). Moreover, even among large-scale farms that routinely use AMS, the marginal impact of fragmented or contiguous land transfer-in on AMS adoption is likely minimal, given their generally high degree of outsourcing throughout the production process.

## Robustness check

For robustness check, we re-evaluate the impact of land transfer-in choices on grain farmers' AMS adoption using a MVTE model based on the augmented inverse probability weighting (AIPW) estimator. Similar to the IPWRA estimator, the AIPW estimator possesses a property of double robustness. When both the selection and outcome equations are correctly specified, the estimates obtained from the two methods converge (Linden et al. 2016). As shown in Appendix Table A1, the results derived from the AIPW estimator are highly consistent with those from the IPWRA estimator, indicating that the empirical results reported in Table 4 are robust.

Meanwhile, considering that the effect of land transfer-in choices on AMS adoption may differ between smallholder farmers and large-scale farms, we winsorize the data at the 1% level based on farm size and re-estimate the MVTE model using the IPWRA estimator. The results, reported in Appendix Table A2, show that if farmers who have not transferred in land were to transfer in fragmented land, their AMS expenditure per mu would increase by 41.4%, whereas transferring in contiguous land would raise their AMS expenditure per mu by 65.2%. These findings are broadly consistent with the empirical results in Table 4, suggesting that extreme outliers did not bias the estimates and that our previous conclusions remain robust.

Therefore, the above results suggest, to some extent that both transferring in contiguous land and increasing AMS adoption are preferable strategies for improving farm performance across farms of different scales. However, for smallholder farmers, fragmented land endowments imply inefficiency and high costs in mechanized production, which increases transaction costs between AMS providers and smallholders. These costs are often passed on to smallholders through service prices (Qiu, Choy, et al. 2021), thereby obscuring the contribution of AMS adoption to farm productivity. This may also explain why the effect of AMS adoption on net returns at the 25th percentile is not statistically significant. In addition, given the considerable disparities in productivity levels among some farmers, we do not examine differences in coefficients across quantiles. Nonetheless, the above results sufficiently demonstrate that contiguous land transfer-in and AMS adoption are of significant importance for smallholders to achieve economies of scale and enhance their productivity.

Shifting from the productivity perspective of farm performance, we reassess farm performance using per mu measures: grain yields per mu (1000 kg/mu) and net returns per mu (1000 yuan/mu), and examine the heterogeneous effects of land transfer-in choices and AMS adoption on the distribution of farm performance from the perspective of production efficiency. As shown in Columns 8 to 13 of Table 6, contiguous and fragmented land transfer-in have statistically significant positive effects on grain yields per mu at the 75th percentile, but not at the 25th and 50th percentiles. As mentioned earlier, farm productivity is closely related to the technological frontier, resource allocation capacity, and operational scale of farmers – the same applies to production efficiency (Abdul-Rahaman, Issahaku, and Zereyesus 2021; Huan, Dong, and Chi 2022; Oumer et al. 2020). Highyield farmers typically face narrower technology gaps, fewer resource constraints, and more pronounced economies of scale. Leveraging these advantages, they can maximize the contribution of land transfer-in to technical efficiency, thereby increasing yields per mu - an outcome that is difficult for smallholders to achieve.

The UQR estimates for the effects of both contiguous and fragmented land transfer-in on net returns per mu are statistically significant and positive. Consistent with earlier findings, the estimated coefficients for contiguous land transfer-in remain larger than those for fragmented land transfer-in, indicating that contiguous land transfer-in also holds a comparative advantage in reducing costs and improving efficiency on farms. Furthermore, the UQR estimates for the effects of AMS adoption on both grain yields per mu and net returns per mu are also statistically significant and positive. Notably, similar to the cases of contiguous and fragmented land transfer-in, farmers with higher grain yields per mu and net returns per mu are more sensitive to AMS adoption. This is largely attributable to the strong alignment between economies of scale and mechanized division of labour (Tufa et al. 2024). Economies of scale emphasize low costs and high efficiency, relying on the decomposition of complex production processes into simple, repetitive, and standardized steps. AMS not only meets these requirements but also helps diversify investment risks in fixed assets, thereby leading to significant gains in production efficiency. Overall, the economies of scale arising from contiguous land transfer-in are realized through AMS adoption, while contiguous land transfer-in in turn promotes AMS adoption. This highlights the complementary relationship between the two in improving farm performance.

## V. Conclusions and policy implications

Previous studies have focused solely on the relationship between land transfer in or out and the adoption of agricultural mechanization services (AMS), which to some extent overlooks the contribution of the continuity of land transferred in. Moreover, there is a lack of discussion on the synergistic relationship between land transfer-in choices (no transfer-in, fragmented transfer-in, and contiguous transfer-in) and AMS adoption. To address this gap, this study utilizes the 2020 China Rural Revitalization Survey data and employs a multivalued treatment effects (MVTE) model and an unconditional quantile regression (UQR) model to account for potential endogeneity. It analyzes the impact of land transfer-in choices on households' AMS adoption and examines the heterogeneous effects of both factors on the distribution of farm performance, measured by grain yields and net returns.

The MVTE estimates indicate that for households without land transfer-in, acquiring fragmented land increases AMS expenditure per mu by 47.4%, while acquiring contiguous land increases it by 63.8%, suggesting that contiguous land transfer-in has a significantly stronger effect on AMS adoption than fragmented land transfer-in. The UQR estimates show that both fragmented and contiguous land transfer-in have significantly positive effects across the distribution of grain yields and net returns, with contiguous land transfer-in exhibiting a more pronounced impact. AMS adoption also significantly and positively affects grain yields and net returns, with stronger effects at higher quantiles. These findings indicate a clear complementary relationship between contiguous land transfer-in and AMS adoption in improving farm performance.

Several policy implications can be derived from these results. First, strengthening the land transfer market to encourage contiguous land transfer-in is essential. Policymakers should continue to improve the legal and institutional framework for clarifying land property rights, standardizing lease contracts, and facilitating contiguous land consolidation. Establishing land information platforms and promoting land parcel mapping could help reduce transaction costs and improve matching efficiency between lessors and lessees. Second, improving the accessibility and affordability of AMS for smallholders remains a policy priority. Since smallholders face both capital and information constraints, the government should enhance public support for AMS adoption by providing targeted subsidies, low-interest credit, and cooperative-based service delivery models. Encouraging the clustering of AMS providers and developing specific mechanization service networks would further strengthen service supply and reduce per unit costs. Third, integrating land transfer and AMS promotion policies could yield synergistic benefits. Policies aimed at consolidating farmland should be coordinated with mechanization service programmes to ensure that land consolidation does not exclude smallholders from mechanization opportunities. Encouraging inclusive AMS provision and supporting collective or cooperative adoption models can enable smallholders to benefit from scale economies without losing access to production resources. Finally, differentiated support strategies should be considered based on farm size and performance characteristics. For smallholders, fragmented land transfer-in still plays a transitional role in promoting AMS adoption and reducing labour burdens. For large-scale farms, contiguous land transfer-in and AMS adoption can jointly improve production efficiency and technological upgrading. Tailoring policy instruments to these heterogeneous needs will contribute to more balanced and sustainable agricultural modernization.

In conclusion, while cross-sectional data may be inadequate for revealing dynamic relationships among variables, it remains sufficient to provide evidence that well-functioning land transfer markets and the expansion of AMS adoption jointly enhance farm performance. Strengthening the integration of these two policy domains is vital for narrowing the productivity gap between smallholder and large-scale farms, and advancing China's rural revitalization and food security goals. Naturally, if the data become available, panel data analysis would help us observe how economies of scale evolve over time, which represents a promising direction for future research.
