## Abstract

A better understanding of the dynamics involved in grain production is critical for food security and poverty reduction. To this end, several factors influence grain production, including capital (i.e. R&D investment and agricultural credit), agriculture environment (i.e. CO₂ emissions), and technical progress (i.e. fertilizers and agricultural machinery). This paper analyses the combined effects of these factors on China's grain crops output from 1990 to 2017 by using the autoregressive distributed lag (ARDL) approach. The ARDL-bounds testing approach provided the long-run connection amid the variables. The findings include: (i) agricultural R&D spending has an encouraging and meaningful influence on grain crops yield in the long-run and short-run; (ii) agricultural credit significantly and positively stimulates grain crops output; (iii) CO₂ emissions negatively impact grains' output in the short- and long-run; (iv) area under grain crops and fertilizers used also endure a positive spur on grain crops production in the long-run and short-run; and (v) short-run nexus among the variables is navigated for robustness rationale via the vector error correction model Granger causality practice. Hence, the strategy specialists should also acquaint finances through prescribed organizations on comfortable provisions and minimal interests. In addition, the Chinese Governments and agricultural extension organizations should support the easy acquisition of high-tech cultivating machinery to harvest crops swiftly and explore substantial markets for further business.

## 1 | INTRODUCTION

Food production has always remained a matter of concern for human beings; however, an exponential increase in population during the 20th century (Guyer et al., 2000) emphasized sustainable food production to ensure food security of 9 billion by 2050 (Manogna & Mishra, 2020). The United Nations sensed the high future demand for food—nearly 60% higher than today—and placed agriculture as the second most important among its listed 2030 Agenda for Sustainable Development Goals (17 SDGs). To this end, global food security has essentially become a challenge to the economy and society both in advanced and developing nations due to plenteous motives, such as heaved population boom, updating tastes, climatic transformation, water dearth, farming as a profession losing interest, and food price volatility (Breene, 2016).

Specifically, China is one of the most densely inhabited nations globally, with 1386 million people (WB, 2017). To feed its population, China increased grain output to 663.84 million tons (MT) in 2019 from 113 MT in 1949 (CSB, 2020); besides, the per capita grain production streamed to 242 kilogram/annum from 209 kg/ annum during the same period (National Bureau of Statistics of China, 2012). This growth in grain production is due to an increased usage of agricultural inputs such as high-quality fertilizers, irrigation, and chemicals (Zhu & Chen, 2002). However, China is still on the verge of food (in)security, wherepeople depend about nearly 20% on grain import from foreign countries. Besides, the population can grow to 1.462 billion by 2030, requiring 775 MT of grains to feed them (Zhang et al., 2019).

The Chinese agricultural sector is challenging and has thoughtful inferences for food storage and economic evolution (Kang et al., 2008). On the supply side, the per hectare yields decreased as supportive policies and technological progress were not present. A few reasons include (i) nonagricultural uses of land, (ii) meagre availability of resources, and (iii) environmental degradation (soil acidification and water contamination). Besides, climatic changes and N-deposition are other crucial challenges China faces (Li et al., 2013; 2014). Therefore, to provide sustainable food supplies and achieve SGDs, it is imperative to undertake the R&D, mobilize agriculture resources, and address the issues of CO2 emission (Belay & Mengiste, 2021; Chandio et al., 2021; De Janvry & Sadoulet, 2009; Paramati et al., 2021).

Similarly, literature provides evidence that the sensible usage of public spending on agriculture has the reasonable capacity to speed up agronomic progress and perk up its productivity (Dkhar & Kumar, 2018). Likewise, Salim and Islam (2010) detailed that agricultural R&D expands the farming output in the long run. For example, public sector expenditure and R&D expenditures in agriculture have a close relationship in the USA economy; from 1990 to 2013, the agricultural and food R&D share was nearly 50% of its total R&D expenditure. This investment increased agricultural productivity in the United States by 169% (\$1.053 trillion in 2017) (Clancy et al., 2016) and made it the world leader in innovation in agriculture (USDA, 2021). Between 2008 and 2011, it produced 54,000 patents in agricultural sciences and 18,000 patents in agriculture (Clancy et al., 2016). These statistics revealed the importance of spending on R&D in agriculture.

Similarly, China undertook several pro-agricultural policies and eight-folded its public spending from \$3707 million in 1990 to \$17,332.5 million in 2017 surpassing the United States in 2008 (Clancy et al., 2016). China also increased its investment in R&D during the last 20 years to become a world leader in sciencetechnology innovation by 2050 (Veugelers, 2017; Băzăvan, 2019). From 1990 to 2018, the R&D expenditures surged from 0.56% of its GDP to 2.18%. In 2018, China produced 16.9% more patents than last year, whereas, in agriculture, 5760 patents were only for the plant varieties—29% more than the previous year (National Bureau of Statistics of China, 2018; WB, 2018). The government disbursed the resources through policy interventions like direct provision imbursement, wideranging subsidizations for procuring agricultural supplies, and seeds and equipment subsidies to enhance food production and farmers' income. These responses of the innovators determined the level of innovation in China and supported agriculture (Chen et al., 2011; Tan, 2010). Figure 1 provides the Gross National Expenditure on R&D by different economies and a swift increase in Chinese expenditures.

Likewise, technology adoption, because of R&D, helps in achieving sustainable growth in agriculture and poverty reduction (Adeola & Ikpesu, 2016; Aliou & Zeller, 2001; Anetor et al., 2016; Diagne, 2002; Diagne & Zeller, 2001; Fall, 2008; Obansa & Maduekwe, 2013). Obansa and Maduekwe (2013) mentions that agriculture credit plays a pivotal role in technology adoption at the farm level; the financing strategy to agriculture helps in the disbursement of technology at the farm level and stimulates sustainable progression (Ruete, 2015). For example, the literature quotes that interest-free loans and supplying farm inputs at subsidized rates can increase (rice) output (Omoregie et al., 2018). Likewise, from 1990 to 2017, China expanded its credit to agriculture from \$241,280 to \$3,080,100 million, which depicts its seriousness toward agricultural development.

On the other hand, China's past development in agriculture and rapid industrialization led to significant energy utilization and CO₂ production. Currently, China is one of the top CO2 emitters globally (9229.8 million tons in 2017), whereas the agriculture sector solely accounts for 17% of the total GHG emission. Further, the researchers have projected that CO2 emissions will surge as agricultural development increases (Hongmin et al., 2008; Xiong et al., 2016; Zhang et al., 2019). Therefore, following the progress of Chinese R&D investment, agricultural credit, CO₂ emissions, and technical progress, it is imperative to investigate their influence on grain production.

To this end, this empirical research examines the combined effects of these factors on China's grain crops output from 1990 to 2017 by using the auto-regressive distributed lag (ARDL) approach. This research provides multiple implications. First, to the authors' best knowledge, this paper makes a novel attempt to study the impacts of R&D spending, agricultural credit, and CO₂ emissions on grain crops output in China in the long- and short-run. Second, the present investigation also emphasizes the long- and short-run impacts of primary input factors such as the area under grain crops, fertilizers used, and agricultural machinery on grain crops production, following the studies of Warsame et al. (2021), Abbas (2021), Ahsan et al. (2020), and Chandio et al. (2021). Third, the empirical analysis uses the DF-GLS unit root test and two long-term approaches (ARDL bounds and the Johansen cointegration tests) to estimate the impacts. The ARDL approach is suitable for current estimation; the method is flexible, appropriate, and can accommodate a mixed integration order among the selected variables. Last, the study utilizes the vector error correction model (VECM) to test the short-run causality connections amid the variables. Given the profoundly discussed background, this study answers the following research questions (RQs):

## 2 | LITERATURE REVIEW AND HYPOTHESES DEVELOPMENT

In agriculture, high yields may result from numerous factors; however, the growing body of literature mentions R&D as a primary foundation for expansion in agricultural productivity (Hall & Scobie, 2006; Salim & Islam, 2010). To this end, the present study examines the effect of R&D expenditure, agricultural credit, and CO2 emissions on crop production in China. Keeping in mind the dependence of agriculture on weather and climate, we also add CO₂ as a proxy for climate change (Antle, 2008). We establish our case based on a literature survey from developed and developing countries, given the above argument.

## 2.1 | Grain crops production and R&D spending nexus

Many researchers used several approaches to assess the drifts of R&D expenditure on economic progression, CO2 emissions, agricultural harvesting yields, and grain crops productivity. Horvath (2011) used a Bayesian Model Averaging to trounce the vagueness and inspected the bonding between (R&D) with long-run economic progression by utilizing the country-level data (1960-1992)

of 72 emerging, under-developing, and advanced countries. Findings revealed a vigorous, constructive drift of R&D on long-term progression. Alene and Coulibaly (2009) observed the stimulation of research on agri-output and poverty calibration in Sub-Saharan Africa (SSA) from 1980 to 2003. They mentioned the substantial role of agronomic expenditures on output growth and an enlarged revenue per capita due to increased output.

Likewise, Guo et al. (2019) analysed the impacts of government spending, financial expansion, and energy utilization on environmental degradation (ED) in Venezuela by undertaking the data from 1971 to 2013. The study pointed out that energy usage impairs environmental quality in the long-and-short run. Moreover, the study pointed out a positive influence of government spending on ecological deterioration, whereas the results mentioned that the financial progression is clogging to the environmental degradation. Hence, the researchers noted that Venezuela's financial institutes could stimulate sustainable energy usage, and the local administration can lower carbon emissions via financial expansion. The scale of government expenditures has a substantial positive influence on real GDP growth, particularly in low developed countries (Bleaney et al., 2001; Fan & Rao, 2003; Loto, 2011). Summing the results mentioned above, the researchers hypothesize:

H1. The R&D spending has a positive impact on grain crops production in China.

## 2.2 | Grain crops production and credit nexus

The credit to the rural sector catalyses the technology adoption at the farm level that moves the production frontier outward (Obansa & Maduekwe, 2013). Many researchers have explored the effect of loaning on agricultural production. To this end, Narayanan (2016) discerned the linkages between credit to the farm sector growth and Indian GDP. He used the panel data from 1995 to 2012 and reckoned a positive effects credit to the agricultural sector on fertilizer intake and mechanization at farms. Similarly, Omoregie et al. (2018) unearthed an encouraging role of finance on rice in Nigeria by undertaking the data from 1981 to 2016. The researchers mentioned that the labour and investment shocks reduce the output. Based on findings, they suggested developing interest-free loan schemes for farmers and appreciating them by supplying farm inputs at subsidized rates.

Another study conducted in Togo by Agbodji and Johnson (2019) testified a favourable impact of agricultural credit on the commodities (Sorghum, Rice, and Maize). However, results varied according to the credit category. Akudugu (2016) investigated the linkages between farming yields, credit accessibility, and land size in Ghana. The results depict the positive and significant association between credit (informal and formal sources) and productivity, where credit from formal sources increases productivity by 0.10%, and informal sources increase productivity by 0.45%. Another micro-level research conducted by Chandio, Jiang, Wei, and Guangshun (2018) in Pakistan inspected the credit interchangeability, credit investment, and credit accessibility of small, medium, and large farmers. Results pointed out that farm production increased with the use of agriculture credit, and consumption levels developed much smoothing.

Afrin et al. (2017) worked on the financial inclusion and proficiency of rice paddy growers in Bangladesh. They unearthed that borrowers were more technically resourceful than non-borrowers. However, they suggested expanding financial inclusion by extending credit to more farmers. Likewise, Chandio, Jiang, Wei, and Guangshun (2018) reported significantly, positively impacts of credit on the wheat crop. Hence, the researchers suggested providing short-term loans for crop-specific intervention.

Conversely, researchers instituted a substantial and constructive influence of financial expansion on agricultural advancement at a macro-level (Shahbaz et al., 2013). The study highlighted supporting the financial sector. Another study conducted by Yazdi and Khanalizadeh (2014) in Iran covers the data from 1970 to 2011, and they found two-way causality between financial expansion and agricultural development. Therefore, in connection with the above discussion, a strong link between agriculture lending and farms production can be developed. Thus, we propose the following hypothesis:

H2. Agricultural credit positively impacts grain crops production in China.

## 2.3  Grain crops production and CO2 emissions nexus

To maintain economic growth, controlling the frequency of greenhouse gas (GHG) emission is a daunting challenge for major developing countries. It is becoming a catastrophic problem due to growth in people and the high use of resources for industrial and agricultural growth (Manogna & Mishra, 2020; Paramati et al., 2021).

Zaied and Cheikh (2015) scrutinized the influence of global climatic changes on crop yields. They used empirical analysis with the application of panel cointegration tests. The results revealed that a higher annual average temperature might reduce the production of cereals and dates, except in uphill areas. The yearly precipitation also has a positive stimulus on cereals; however, rain's absence adversely influences output in the southern region. Furthermore, the short-run effects of climate were less than the long-run effects. Chang (2002) inspected the effects of global climatic changes on 60 crop produce in Taiwan. Empirical findings suggest that climate change and warming have a significant but nonmonotonic effect on productivity.

Furthermore, Lobell (2007) concluded a harmful effect of temperature on paddy and maize producing nations. Welch et al. (2010) assessed the drifts of everyday lowest and highest temperatures on rice crops in tropical Asia. By employing the statistics on daily temperature and paddy crop in six rice-producing nations, they retrieved statistically substantial influences of temperature and radioactivity on the rice roots. Alam et al. (2011) studied the long-run connection of energy utilization and carbon emissions with revenue for the Indian economy by undertaking the data from 1971 to 2006. The findings depicted the presence of long-run two-way causality between energy utilization and carbon emissions.

Ozturk and Salah Uddin (2012) investigated the long-run connection between CO₂ emissions, energy use, and economic advancement in India from 1971 to 2007 by employing the Johansen cointegrating technique and causality test. The causality disclosed the path of causation between carbon emissions and economic development. Further, Tiwari et al. (2013) reviewed the linkages between India's economic advancement, coal usage, trade openness, and carbon dioxide emissions from 1966 to 2009. The results depicted the existence of long-run association links among the variables. Empirical evidence also validated the EKC hypothesis in the short and long run. The causality investigation indicated the feedback hypothesis among economic progression and carbon emissions, and the same conclusion was drawn among the consumption of coal and CO₂ emissions. Furthermore, trade openness Granger causes economic development, CO₂ emissions, and coal consumption.

Qureshi et al.'s (2016) study in Pakistan found the effects of fossil fuel energy, greenhouse gas emissions (GHS), and airborne pollution on crop production from 1980 to 2013. The findings exposed that CO₂ emissions positively impacted sugarcane and wheat crop yields, while GHG emissions sternly shook Pakistan's wheat, cotton, and rice crop production. Similarly, Saidi and Hammami (2015) checked the impacts of economic advancement and carbon emissions on energy use for 58 countries by undertaking the data of 1990-2012 and employing the GMM procedure. The outcomes unearthed the significantly positive impact of CO₂ emissions on the level of energy usage.

## 4.1  Descriptive statistics and correlation analysis

In determining the effect of undertaken explanatory variables on grain production in China, the first step of this investigation is to check the descriptive statistics of the study variables. Table 2 provides detailed results of 28 observations of the undertaken variables. The study variables, including lnGP, lnR&D, lnCR, lnCO₂, lnAR, lnFR, and lnAGM, are normally distributed as exhibited by the Jarque-Bera test and the similar values of mean and median. Figure 4 depicts the trend of the study variables from 1990 to 2017. Furthermore, Table 3 reports the results of the correlation matrix, indicating that agricultural R&D investment (lnR&D), agricultural credit (lnCR), carbon dioxide emissions (lnCO₂), area under grain crops (lnAR), fertilizers consumption (lnFR), and agricultural machinery (lnAGM) significantly, positively correlate with grain crops production (lnGP) during the period 1990–2017.

## 4.2  Unit root test results

This paper utilized the Dickey-Fuller generalized least squares (DF-GLS) unit root method to evaluate and determine the integration levels of study variables (Elliott et al., 1992). The results also produced trend and intercept, as shown in Table 4. The findings depict that the agricultural R&D investment (lnR&D) is stationary at level I(0) at the 1% level of significance, while grain crops production (lnGP), agricultural credit (lnCR), carbon dioxide emissions (lnCO₂), area under grain crops (lnAR), fertilizers consumption (lnFR), and agricultural machinery (lnAGM) are stationary at first-difference I (1) at the 1%, 5%, and 10% level of significance.

## 4.3  Lag selection for ARDL-ECM

After achieving the stationarity of the data sets, the next step of this investigation is to find out the optimum lag length for long-term cointegration estimation. To this end, the study employed the AIC (Akaike, 1974) and the SIC. Table 5 reports the outcomes of lag order selection standards, which indicate that a more appropriate lag value is lag 2 for ARDL-ECM estimation. Both lag selection criteria that is, Akaike information criterion (AIC) and Schwarz information criterion (SIC), are commonly used for the modelling. The present investigation utilizes the final ARDL (1, 0, 0, 1, 0) model based on AIC.

## 4.4 | ARDL bound testing approach and robustness analysis

The present paper utilizes the ARDL-bound testing method for inspecting the cointegration (Zhang et al., 2017). Table 6 reports the estimated outcomes of the cointegration test. The calculated F-statistics that is, 7.69, 5.41, 4.96, 21.96, 7.08, 9.27, and 5.08 exceed the upper bounds (UB) at 1% significance level when grain crops production (lnGP), agricultural R&D investment (lnR&D), agricultural credit (lnCR), carbon dioxide emissions (lnCO₂), area under grain crops (lnAR), fertilizers consumption (lnFR), and agricultural machinery (lnAGM) are used as dependent variables. Thus, this paper confirms the presence of a long-term linkages among grain crops production (lnGP), agricultural R&D investment (lnR&D), agricultural credit (lnCR), carbon dioxide emissions (lnCO2), area under grain crops (lnAR), fertilizers consumption (lnFR), and agricultural machinery (lnAGM) in the case of China.

## 4.6  VECM estimates validation

The last step of this paper is to determine the causal association among study variables. Table 9 provides the detailed results by validating the short-run causal associations under the VECM short-run methodology. The findings are impactful for strategists. The results showed that only credit to agriculture and fertilizers consumption has significant bidirectional causation. In contrast, agricultural machinery has uni-directional causality running from agricultural machinery usage to CO₂ emission.

Additionally, the two-path causality exists between carbon emission and fertilizers consumption, carbon emission and credit to grain crops productivity, and carbon emission and area under crops production. However, one-path causality is also present between area under crops production, agricultural machinery, credit to agriculture sector, and fertilizers consumption. ## 5 | CONCLUSION AND POLICY IMPLICATIONS

This investigation discovers the long-run causal nexus between R&D spending, CO₂ emissions, credit to agriculture, and grain crops output in the context of China over the period 1990–2017. The area under grain crops, usage of fertilizers, and agricultural machinery were taken as additional determining factors. The long-run causal connection was estimated using the ARDL bounds testing method. The DF-GLS unit root test indicated that agricultural R&D spending is stationary at I(0), whereas the output of grain crops, agricultural credit, CO₂ emissions, area under grain crops, usage of fertilizers, and agricultural machinery are stationary at I(1).

The empirical results of the ARDL bounds testing of the cointegration technique offer the presence of the long-run cointegration between the grain crops output, R&D spending, agricultural credit, CO₂ emissions, area under grain crops, fertilizers used, and agricultural machinery. The long- and short-run estimations determined that R&D expenditure and credit to the agricultural sector positively contributed to grains production, while CO₂ emissions negatively affected grain crops output in the long- and short-run. Moreover, fertilizers used and area under grain crops positively influenced grain crops output in long andshort-run.

On the other hand, agricultural equipment significantly improved grain crops output in the short run and negatively influenced grain crops output in the long run. The short-run connection among all the study variables is also investigated with the VECM method. The estimated results reveal comprehensive causation among study variables, which also signified that the Chinese government should develop dynamic plans and policies to reduce the reliance on fossil fuel consumption in the agricultural sector and easy access to formal credit from one window operation.

Our empirical estimations also mentioned that the increase in R&D expenditures, easy access to formal credit, and agricultural machinery (e.g. tractors, tube wells, and sowing machinery) play a vital role in aggregating the scale of crops in China. Consequently, this study endorses that formal credit to the rural sector should enable farmers to acquire modern machinery in instalments. Moreover, the state's rural financial institutions e.g. China Agricultural Bank should innovate and restructure their loaning products to achieve sustainable agriculture productivity growth.

Based on the given estimation, we can also understand the significance of the nexus between R&D and agriculture output, notably when climatic factors create uncertainty and enhance risk of crop production. The policymakers should devise a sustainable agriculture policy for the future food needs of China's population. Moreover, a stimulative policy to scientific research and innovation may increase the returns of R&D expenditure on food production.

The strategy specialists should also allow more institutions to lend money at minimal interest rates to the agricultural sector. Further, the Chinese governments and agriculture extension system should support the easy acquisition of high-tech agriculture machinery to harvest crops swiftly and explore substantial markets for further business. Additionally, the researchers should probe the effect of fiscal and monetary policies and temperature variation due to the climatic changes (extreme and lowest temperature and rainfall) on key harvesting output by using the national and multnational datasets.
