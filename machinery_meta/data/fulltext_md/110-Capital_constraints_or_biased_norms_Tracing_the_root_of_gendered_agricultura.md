## ABSTRACT

Notwithstanding the voluminous localized evidence of great gender divergence in agricultural productivity, the intricacies and interactions in underlying mechanisms—from comparative advantage at capitals and capabilities to biased social norms and institutions—lack a systematic quantification. This study applies a gender-inclusive livelihood framework, integrating feminist economics and simultaneous equation systems, to unveil the root causes and the role of gender norms in perpetuating agricultural inequality. Drawn on 12795 rural households in China from 2015 to 2019, our findings dismantle a resource-contingent decision-making process, interacted with local institutions, in constituting 7–13 % gender-productivity gaps. Earlier-born cohorts suffer concentrated yield gaps reaching 23 %. While limited access to fundamental resources and economic opportunities for rural women explains 62–82 % of the path-based mechanism, pervasive inequalities in human capital and access to crop marketing channels emerge as predominant contributors, stressing long-standing gaps in knowledge and motivation. Local gender-biased norms further exacerbate gender-productivity gaps by undermining returns to women's endowments, and an intra-household old-age caring penalty disproportionately restricts their market participation. We conclude with lessons for optimizing gender-transformative intervention frameworks.

## 1. Introduction

Global agri-food system has witnessed both managerial and labor feminization since 1980 (Asadullah and Kambhampati, 2021), characterized by a consistent increase of women's role and labor contribution in agriculture. This widespread “Feminization of Agriculture" evolves with fast-paced urbanization and male-dominated rural out-migration, triggering a long-lasting shift in traditional gender roles and power relations within rural areas (Zhang et al., 2004; Leder, 2022). As reported by Food and Agriculture Organization of the United Nations (FAO) in 2011, women comprise 43 % of the agricultural workforce in developing countries, and this value exceeds 50 % in Sub-Saharan Africa and South Asia.

Women have also experienced growing autonomy and dominance in multi-scale farm decision-making (Mahajan, 2019). The percentage of women farm operators ranges from 4 to 18 % globally (FAO, 2011), while in China, around 13 % of farms were managed by women at the household level (De Brauw et al., 2008). Hence, to free up women trapped by an inequitable distribution of productive resources and agricultural income is a significant pathway towards livelihood empowerment of women food growers.

The marginalized gender and class suffer a growing livelihood vulnerability under an interactive convergence of multiple global challenges—involving food insecurity, income segregation, and poverty concentration—following neo-liberalization in the global economy and the food trade. Despite noticeable gender convergence over the last century, there still exists a globally pervasive gender gap in peasants' productivity performance, ranging from 4 % to 40 % across Sub-Saharan Africa and South Asia (Kilic et al., 2015; Ali et al., 2016; Mahajan, 2019), and has substantially affected rural women's welfare and the overall socioeconomic development. A series of country-specific studies between the mid-1990s and 2020s quantitatively examined gender differentials in agricultural productivity within African smallholder systems (Hirpa Tufa et al., 2021; De la O Campos et al., 2016; Ali et al., 2016; Kilic et al., 2015; Slavchevska, 2015; Aguilar et al., 2015). The picture expanded to the context of South Asia in more recent studies, shedding light on comparative analyses of global feminized agriculture (Mishra et al., 2017; Mahajan, 2019).

Debates in the literature suggest that the dominant constraints faced by farm women and the mechanisms of gender-productivity gaps could differ across various contexts. Among the existing research, the observed gendered agricultural productivity is mainly attributed to farming behavioral differentials, estimated with separated, unrestricted equations that ignores potential recursive pathways. Such an identification brings imbalanced access to land asset ownership and input materials as well as input market imperfections to the fore (Palacios-López and López, 2015). Whereas the role of unequal livelihood capabilities and economic opportunities, under persistent influences of discriminatory/egalitarian gender norms, have not been fully recognized. A systematic identification of root causes underlying gender-productivity disparities prompt a rethinking of investment returns in women-targeted agricultural interventions.

This paper on farm-level gendered agricultural productivity and its underlying mechanisms, with new localized evidence from China, contributes to the causes debates by addressing the following questions: (1) Robust measures of gaps: How does gender-specific productivity evolve across years and birth cohorts? (2) The intricacies of determinants: How does a gender-unjust distribution of capitals, capabilities, and social aspirations intrinsically interact to shape disparities in agricultural decision-making and productivity? (3) The mainstay in mechanisms: What are the root causes that dominantly exacerbate genderproductivity gaps or contribute the most in bridging this disparity? (4) The moderating role of gender norms: How do gender-biased beliefs and aspirations reshape the returns to endowments and the determinative roles of gender-asset gaps, and, what is the distribution of structural inequality across generations and household structures? While some ambiguities in the literature might endure due to different ways in which variables are measured, a path-based method of simultaneous equation systems applied to penetrate mechanisms and a new angle of viewgender norms as a local institutional moderator—integrated into analysis are expected to provide new insights to the literature.

With detailed data from nationwide rural survey about farm households and agricultural production, the study theoretically integrate a feminist perspective into the comprehensive livelihood framework developed by Scoones (1998) and Ashley and Carney (1999), which emphasizes interactions of various livelihood components. The intricate decision-making dynamics of smallholders contingent on capitals and capabilities are quantified through fixed-effect panel regressions and structural equation modelling. Our results lay an emphasis on the role of knowledge and motivation, including gender gaps in education attainment, market access, and machinery adoption. Strengthened by gender-biased norms, women are observed to receive lower returns to resource endowments, aggregating into declined agricultural technical efficiency and total factor productivity. This also serves as a structural element of gender-productivity gap.

This paper makes three contributions to the existing literature. First, to our best knowledge, this is the first article to decode the intermediary pathways of gendered agricultural productivity and identify the root causes that perpetuate this inequality. Prior literature underestimates the recursive influences of basic capitals and capabilities in elucidating gender differentials, using unrestricted estimates of production functions and decomposition methods (Mahajan, 2019; Ali et al., 2016; Slavchevska, 2015; Kilic et al., 2015). Such approaches disregard the interactive decision-making dynamics inherent in gendered behavioral patterns. This paper adds to the literature by employing simultaneous equation systems that treat land management, crop choice, and input use as endogenous mediators, based on which the role of each contributor and channel is calculated. The principal methodological problem faced in establishing a causality is the potential endogeneity within mechanisms. Considering spurious correlations, we include multi-level confounds and a series of fixed effects to rule out potential confounding effects for more plausible causal claims.

Second, this article provides an evidence-based investigation of how local cultural norms and institutional environments indirectly affect intergenerational evolutions of gender-productivity gaps. Prior literature has recognized the significant role of social institutions as unexplained structural effects accounting for 30–80 % of the gap, with an absence of quantitative analyses documenting how regional-cohort variations in institutional factors reshape gender convergence in agriculture. Gender norms are social expectations towards the role of women and men. Conservative gender norms always exhibit discriminatory beliefs. We construct provincial and village-level indicators measuring women's status and the conservativeness of gender norms, and examine how it might reinforce or undermine the role of resource determinants across different birth cohorts and household structures. A semi-parametric decomposition method is further integrated to visualize distributions of structural inequality over agricultural productivity (Mahajan, 2019). Another set of indicators refer to domestic caring burden from the old-age and the child, measuring a “caring penalty" that disproportionately restrict rural women's mobility and market participation.

Third, the paper answers the question of where the returns to interventions aimed at gender convergence would be the highest, raised by Doss et al. (2018). Achieving gender equality in agriculture emphasizes (re)targeted agricultural interventions for women's empowerment. Nevertheless, the literature shows that smallholders' decisions on input usage are not just a matter of financial and material conditions (Heckert et al., 2023). Karamba and Winters' (2015) research on an input subsidy program in Malawi demonstrates that such a design enhances overall productivity without disproportionately helping women. Hence, we perform a counterfactual policy simulation to compare the effective returns between different intervention pathways. The results re-emphasize the role of targeted investments in women's market connection within agri-food value chains in bridging the gender-productivity gaps, rather than direct subsidies on constrained productive inputs. Our analysis provides a catalyst to rethink women's motivation and capability to participate in and benefit from gender-sensitive agricultural interventions.

## 2. Theoretical framework: capitals, capabilities, and contexts

Rules governing patterns of resources allocation are of fundamental importance in determining the distribution of income and productivity within a society. The Sustainable Livelihood Framework (SLF), built on a people-centered approach, advances an agro-ecological understanding of household farming strategies that are dependent on capitals and capabilities, collectively interacted with institutions and power structures (Manuel et al., 2023). Smallholder agriculture is fundamentally self-controlled at the family unit with a reliance on internal resources and an autonomy from market profit-orientation, as a subsistence or reproductive livelihood (Bernstein et al., 2018; van der Ploeg, 2013). Capturing the complexity of agricultural livelihoods, the SLF places local knowledge, practice, and institutions of smallholders at the center, shifting monetary focus towards household constraints and means of living (Sevilla Guzmán and Woodgate, 2013). This enables the application of the SLF for analyzing peasant economics.

The framework dismantles the role of livelihood capitals into material and social assets under the influences of institutional and policy environment (Scoones, 1998; Morse et al., 2009). Following this analytical paradigm, we delve into the question of (1) whether gendered farm productivity can be explained by unequal resource allocation and capital constraints, and (2) how the inequality is further exacerbated by gender-biased local norms. The SLF has integrated new institutional economic thinking since Iran Scoones (1998) that emphasizes the reshaping role of ‘institutions and power relations’ (Natarajan et al., 2022), promoting reorientation towards analysis of structural inequality underlying rural poverty, and thus contributing to the debates of empowerment and gender inequality. Rural households and communities need an intersectional lens of gender relations—used to be blurred as background noise'—to decode clusters of identities and examine how social roles influence resource constraints and opportunities (Manuel et al., 2023).

To capture gender dynamics across farm households, a perspective of feminist economics is integrated into different dimensions of SLF to penetrate gender inequalities in farming strategies and productivities (Fig. 1).¹ In rural agrarian contexts, control over arable land reinforces the persistence of patriarchal bargains and labor divisions, while feminist economics bring issues of justice in rural society to the fore by centering women's situated knowledge and embodied practices. We draw on feminist perspective to take a critical look at gendered aspirations, status, and power in local norms as well as women's interconnected roles as laborers and caregivers.

## 2.1. Formal and informal institutional processes

Political processes and formal institutions, including policies, regulations, and administrations, shape an external driver impacting rural livelihoods. It plays a direct role in reallocating resources by subsidizing inputs or unveiling women-targeted credits. Indirectly, it may reconfigure traditional gender roles and reshape power relations. Within food and agricultural sector, there is a pressing need to establish a costeffective gender intervention framework, specifically targeting at marginalized and disempowered women. At present, a mere 19 % of agricultural policies globally reflect a genuine gender-sensitive approach. The majority of rural development policies acknowledge women's role in agriculture without prioritizing the target of bridging inequality (FAO, 2023), yielding a gendered return consequently (Karamba and Winters, 2015). Recent studies posit that top-down interventions should integrate an angle from “below" to recenter women's knowledge, agency, and capacity to benefit from development policy (Eissler et al., 2020; Gartaula et al., 2025).

The informal social structures, as another aspect of institutional processes, involve discriminatory norms and market imperfections that (re)shape livelihood choices (Natarajan et al., 2022). This element represents the society aspirations and serves as a repository of collective knowledge (Morse et al., 2009; Nandi and Nedumaran, 2021). It manifests itself in gendered roles/responsibilities and perpetuates power imbalances, discrimination, and stereotypes. The social belief systems impose a disproportionate unpaid domestic burden on women, dampening their aspirations for personal development (Kosec and Mo, 2017; Elias et al., 2018). Institutional context dynamically interacts with all other elements, reinforcing a systematic inequality. Gender-biased institutions incur gendered exposure to risks, particularly in contexts where women's mobility and time-use agency are restricted, and condition their capacity to access and benefit from resources. Specifically, discriminatory rules of exchange also influences women's participation in marketing and other profit-oriented activities (Crossland et al., 2021). Accordingly, we propose the first hypothesis as follows:

Hypothesis 1. Gender-biased institutions exacerbate gender inequality in agricultural productivity: Women receive lower returns to resource endowments under discriminatory local norms.

## 2.2. Gender-asset gap in basic livelihood endowments and opportunities

Gender disparity in livelihood resources and how it influences productivity by shaping farming strategies are the mainstay of our analysis. To tailor the framework for analyzing smallholder farm, we reinterpret the gender-asset gaps from perspectives of human capital (i.e., education and health), financial capital (i.e., credit and financial asset), access to extension service, and market opportunity.

Schooling and agricultural extension serve as principle human capital investments for smallholders. Farmers' literacy and social cognition determine their capability to access, interpret and benefit from information on financial services, market dynamics, and technical practices (Ngimwa et al., 1997). Education attainment is intricately linked to the social status and further development of women, widely perceived as an important way to decenter male-dominated power relations and empower women in decision-making processes. Nevertheless, it's still far from gender convergence in enrollment and literacy within rural areas of the global South. Furthermore, as posited by Trauger et al. (2008), women are at a disadvantage in rural knowledge systems, both in top-down transfer model centered at “experts" and in participatory networks. Women have long been socially constructed as “farmwives" or “farm helpers" with a femineity-symbolized role in labor division, putting women in a position deviated from the central focus of education systems (Manuel et al., 2023).

Financial and credit market imperfections have been widely acknowledged as driving factors of gender disparities in agricultural productivity (Julien et al., 2023; Kilic et al., 2015; Palacios-López and López, 2015). Rural women face disparities in accessing bank accounts and financial services, thereby impeding their ability to make upfront investments, manage operational risks, and capitalize on market opportunities (Boucher et al., 2008). Empirical evidence from earlier research indicates that female-headed households were 200 % more credit constrained while paying higher interest on loans than dual-headed households (Lawal and Muyiwa, 2009; Foltz et al., 2000).

Additionally, women's access to market opportunities in agricultural value chains continues to significantly lag behind that of men, thus undermining their incentives to employ profit-oriented strategies and engage in commercial agriculture with an imagination of modernity. Previous research has emphasized women's limited market participation in rice, barley, sorghum, and sugar schemes in SSA (Schneider and Gugerty, 2010). Gender disparities in commercialization are shaped by capabilities in farm efficiency and risk mitigation. As constrained by credits, income and assets, women are more vulnerable to market shocks (Djurfeldt et al., 2018), and limited access to education, extension service, internet and social networks further undermine their ability to access, process and predict market information. Hill and Vigneri (2014) employ transaction costs as a framework to elucidate gendered nature of market access, emphasizing the role of factors such as distance and information. Compressing market transaction costs depends not only on better infrastructure, but also on adequate knowledge and experience of marketing. Discriminatory institutions and gender biases further erode women's ability to negotiate and bargain for contracts. To wrap up, we propose the following hypothesis:

Hypothesis 2. Gender-productivity gaps in agriculture are directly shaped by resource-contingent farming strategies: Women suffer systematic disadvantages in control over and returns to basic material and social capitals, which dominates the emergence of agricultural gender inequality.

## 2.3. Gendered patterns of farm management

Gendered access to and usage of agricultural inputs, involving land, labor, technology and materials, has been the focus of previous research. Julien et al. (2023) emphasized the role of land and labor market imperfections in shaping gender-productivity gap with data from Malawi, Tanzania, and Uganda. Backiny-Yetna and McGee (2015) attributed it to gender disparities in land ownership, fertilizer use, and labor supervision in the Nigerian context. Existing research primarily focuses on land ownership and property rights (Komatsu et al., 2022; Yokying and Lambrecht, 2020), overlooking gendered decisions on land rental and abandonment.

In rural China, the land property system, based on a separation of ownership rights, contract rights, and management rights, allows smallholders to reallocate farmland while retaining contract rights (Deininger and Jin, 2005). Rural women, burdened with caregiving and constrained by productive resources, are often compelled to renting out or abandoning portions of contracted land. These rental arrangements serve to alleviate their credit and financial constraints (Chen et al., 2023; Komatsu et al., 2022). Rural household's decisions on land rental can be modeled with a utility function, as smallholders are hypothesized to maximize their utility with resource constraints (Manski, 1977). ## 3. Data and summary statistics

## 3.1. Data sources

To estimate farm-level productivity disparities under de facto male and female headship, we utilize the China Rural Household Panel Survey (CRHPS) data from Chinese Family Database (CFD). The CRHPS is a nationally representative, biannual longitudinal survey of rural communities, households, and individuals, launched by the Social Science Experimental Center of Zhejiang University. It covers 29 provinces, autonomous regions and municipalities in China. At the household level, the CRHPS contains characteristics about family structure, employment, agricultural production, land use and transfer, financial behavior, education, and social security. For our study, we collect data from the latest 3 waves (2015, 2017, 2019) and exclude households without engagement in grain or cash crop cultivation, which finally consists of a representative sample of 21,432 rural households in China.

To investigate the role of gender identity norms, we employ the 2010 wave of the Survey on Chinese Women's Social Status (SCWSS). It consists of nine categories: health, education, economy, politics, social security, lifestyle, marriage and family, legal rights and cognition, and gender awareness and attitude. The sampling framework of the SCWSS is representative at the provincial level and allows for a measurement of regional variations of underlying gender norms.

## 3.2. Variables and measures

Agricultural productivity outcomes: The outcome variable includes 1) land and labor productivity, calculated by log-transforming the total yield value per hectare (or per man-day) over all crops and plots cultivated by each household, and 2) Total Factor Productivity (TFP) accounting for endowment differentials in farm input intensity, which allows for a comparison of technical efficiency (returns on inputs) between genders and better measures the net marginal effect of livelihood resources on the efficiency disparities. The TFP is estimated in a Cobb-Douglas production function regressing on land productivity with input intensity to relax assumption of constant returns to farm scale, which is specified as follows:

Alternative measurements include technological gap ratio and technical efficiency that quantify gender-specific managerial efficiency at the stochastically estimated best-practice frontier.

Household-level gender indicator: The gender indicator is captured by a dummy variable, with 1 representing de facto femaleheaded households where women dominate the family decisionmaking, potentially resulted from male out-migration or marital change (widowhood, divorce and singleness). The survey question asking for the household head emphasizes that the headship recorded here refers to the primary decision-maker for family affairs instead of the name registered as the head, which allows for a more pinpoint and robust analysis. While practices of having separated plot managers are common in Western Africa³ (De la O Campos et al., 2016), China's policy traditions and cultural background have converged the decision-making process within a household. A 40-year implementation of an enduring household responsibility system, alongside policies aimed at addressing tenure fragmentation, have reshaped resource allocation patterns in rural China. Rural lands, contracted on a household basis, underscore the household as the pivotal unit in decision-making for smallholder agriculture. The literature analyzes gender dynamics within and across households, while an inter-household analysis is more commonly used in the case of Asian countries (Mishra et al., 2017; Mahajan, 2019). Similarly, China's smallholder systems are characterized by household-based land allocation and family collaboration on cross-plot farming management. There's usually one dominant manager in a farm household who solely makes decisions or consults with the spouse.

Mechanism variables: We introduce well-designed mediators into regressions for quantifying the determinative role of capital constraints, including gender-specific resource endowments (human and financial capital, access to extension service, channels for crop marketing, and internet exposure), land management (farm size under cultivation, land rentals, and land abandonment), cropping choice on portfolios of food and cash crops (Slavchevska, 2015; Mahajan, 2019), and input use decisions on farm machinery, labor (family labor and hired labor), and materials (fertilizer, pesticide, plastic films, etc). Considering potential endogeneity, we focus on years of schooling and access to public credits as proxies of possession of human and financial capitals. The effects of resource endowments are measured at both (1) intensive and (2)

extensive margin—from a perspective of justice in access—and are robust to alternative indicators (Table 1, Panel B).

Gender norm indicators: Provincial gender norm indicators are constructed based on eight questions of the SCWSS representing the conservativeness of gender attitudes, such as “Men are inherently more capable than women", "Men should focus on society, and women should focus on family", “Earning money to support family is males' responsibility rather than females", “The prospect of husbands outweighs wives", and “For women, marring well outweighs doing well in careers" (1–5 scores). Focusing on rural societies, we exploit samples located in rural regions. The scores of each question is z-scoring normalized and aggregated at the provincial level with equal weights. As high scores refer to gender-biased perception, we categorize provinces with a value exceeding zero into conservative institutional contexts with patriarchal norms. At the village level,4 collected from the CRHPS, we use measurements of female political representation as proxies of local women's status, including female village leader (=1) and female membership in the Chinese Communist Party (ratio). At the household level, with rural ageing dominating the demographic transition, providing care to the old-age with deteriorating health status may substantially aggravate women's workloads. And we construct elderly dependency ratio to examine the “care penalty" mechanism on rural women's market participation.

## 4.1.2. Structural equation modeling (SEM)

To make progress uncovering interactive mechanisms shaping gendered agricultural productivity, we construct a hierarchical simultaneous equation model, technically based on covariance matrix,6 to estimate recursive relationships between variables for a path-through analysis (Bollen, 1989). Following the theoretical livelihood framework and proposed hypotheses (H2–3), we accordingly specify the structural equation model for a data-driven validation. Female-managed farms are invested with significantly lower levels of non-land inputs. After controlling for confounds, there still exists a 17- percent of gender-input gap in the overall intensity. Farm machinery critically embodies gender disparity. Results show a 68-percent of gender gap remained in machine use after including controls, compared with 150-percent of mean difference. Brandth (1995) posits that farm machinery has been socially defaulted as an extension of masculinity with strength. Household input strategies respond interactively to resource endowments. A 1-year average increase in schooling is associated with a 4.6 % increase in values of machine input. As our data reconfirms that women face market constraints and tend to farm for home consumptions, its discourages them to hire laborers and invest more on farms.

Table 3 presents changes in the remaining gender-productivity gap after accounting for resource endowments. The raw gap in land productivity is 12.7 %, while an inclusion of resource endowments and regional fixed effects offset 3.4 % of the gap. Further controlling for different levels of labor, machine, and materials input, we find a 7 % of gender gap in agricultural TFP, underscoring the role of technical efficiency gaps rather than unequal access to farm inputs. Gender-asset gaps in livelihood endowments account for one third (2.4 %) of the TFP gaps, with an unexplained 4.6 % of gender gaps remained. The overall endowment-input effects explain 7.6-percent (62 %) of the raw productivity gaps on female- and male-dominated farms of the same area, reconfirming that gender inequality in kernel supply-driven resources and capabilities are the roots of the underlying mechanism. Additional checks drawn on Heckman two-stage regressions validate the robustness of our estimations after integrating an IMR in column (5) and (10). Another aspect to highlight is the estimated lower technical efficiency on women-dominated farms (Fig. 5). Comparing observed yields to gender-specific best-practice frontier, we find a gender gap of 0.018 (±0.004) in managerial performance, which expands to 0.027 (±0.003) when accounting for technology gaps at the meta-production frontier.

Using subsets for estimating gender-specific input use efficiency, Table 4 suggests that male smallholders gain higher rate of returns to additional one-unit input—particularly for pesticides—than female smallholders. Gender gaps in returns to cultivating cash crops reach 18.8 %, which may be rooted in women's lower level of technical knowledge and profit-oriented incentive for more refined farm management. Comparing farm-size elasticity, a 1 % increase in cultivated area results in a 0.55 % and 0.51 % decrease in land productivity on female- and male-dominated farms, while in column (3) only hired labor inputs are significantly correlated with productivity, indicating a higher productivity discount and an increasing reliance on hired labor for women operating large-scale farms. Previous research attributes it to labor capacity constraints under market discrimination, including the quantity and quality of laborer and trouble in supervision (De la O Campos et al., 2016; Pierotti et al., 2022).

Furthermore, we analyze evolving gender-productivity gaps with different household-based endowments and preferences, as well as their intergenerational trends. Fig. 6 depict coefficient trajectories of gendergroup interaction terms, suggesting that women smallholders receive higher returns to public credit access (Fig. 6a). Nevertheless, when cultivating cash crops or located in villages that have implemented land certification, gender-productivity gaps are even exacerbated, indicating a potential gender-biased property right institutions. Considering time and cohort effects, Fig. 6b show a concentrated distribution of inequality among earlier birth cohorts before 1975, compared to the 1985–1990 cohort. After controlling cohort fixed effects, an enlarging trend in productivity gaps remains from 2015 to 2019, which provides future insights on rural gender inequality.

## 5.1.2. The moderating role of local gender-biased norms

Evaluating the role of institutional environment in three-way interaction models, we find that regional gender norms have great potential to exacerbate agricultural inequality by (re)shaping gender-specific capital returns. Table 5 demonstrates that in provinces where people hold more conservative attitudes towards gender roles, women suffer stronger systematic disadvantages in receiving equal returns to resource endowments, especially for households with unpaid caregiving burden for the elderly or the children. Cohorts with birth years before 1950 or within 1970–1990 experience stronger influences of local gender-biased norms. Our findings are robust to using provincial sex ratio of the second childbirth as an alternative proxy of regional norms,8 which consistently reflects the moderating role of gender discrimination.

Table 6 presents dynamics in gendered capital returns varying on village-level female political representation and social status. When village governance and political institutions are led and dominated by females, we observe a significant increase in returns to women's access to marketing channels, especially for the cohort with birth years between 1970 and 1990. This further emphasizes the linkage between gendered politics, bargaining power, and economic independence.

## 5.2. Decoding mechanisms: influencing pathways of gendered agricultural productivity

Fig. 7 depicts a cross-sectional path analysis using survey data of the latest 2019 wave. Standardized path coefficients providing a measure of relative strength of interactions, testified to be well-fitting with data (Tab. A6). Contributions in Tab. A7 reveal that a significant portion of cross-path total effects, amounting to 67 %, can be attributed to indirect effects stemming from resource endowments and farm management. It is also noteworthy that structural inequality embodied in socially constructed gender relations, through a direct unexplained pathway, account for 33 % of the overall effects.

Indirect effects from farm size account for 15–34 % of total effects by reshaping gender-specific input intensity and productivity. Larger farm size shows inverse relationships with input intensity and land productivity, except machinery use. Women's smaller farm size, on average, offsets 52 % of the land productivity gaps, but hampers the capacity to improve yield returns to non-land input.9Another aspect to highlight is that the deadlocks in women's input intensity should be attributed to limited educational and marketing experience rather than financial constraint.

By integrating three waves (2015–2019) of data, we estimate the real effects of each pathway linking gender and agricultural productivity, conditional on household socio-economic characteristics and village institutions. Contributions of direct and indirect effects—categorized by the first mediator in the pathway—are calculated in Fig. 8 and Table 7. The indirect effects via gendered education attainment account for 60 % of net effects and 19 % of total effects on gender-productivity gap. It recursively drives gender disparities in the use of machinery and other input, with a relative contribution of 20 % in net effects. Equipping female farmers with high-quality education could prompt their responses to machinery adoption and labor substitution towards technicalintensive farming (Brandth, 2006).

Table 7 shows that acquirements of agricultural credits is an important mediator of gendered farm size, but exerts little net effects on differentiated input intensity and land productivity, which may due to gaps in knowledge about financial institutions and markets (Fletschner and Mesbah, 2011). By supporting upfront investments and alleviate budget constraints, credit availability is proved to incentivize decisions of large-scale farming by alleviating monetary deadlocks in land circulation (Adelino et al., 2025).

About 20–30 % of total effects on agricultural input and productivity are direct effects of gender, which account for 50–70 % of gender gaps in farm size and machinery use. Female-managed farms with lower output are more vulnerable to discriminatory institutions, restricting them to acquire equal returns to resources and inputs.

## 5.3. Policy simulations: empowering women to bridge gender-yield disparities

To quantify how bridging gender-resource inequality may offset yield gaps, we perform an out-of-sample linear prediction to simulate women-targeted interventions pathways, combing estimates from both fixed-effects models and the SEM. We first intersect the role of capital constraints with provincial gender norms by dividing samples into 2 subsets located in conservative or egalitarian local society. Fig. 10a separately estimate dynamics in gender-yield gaps with each subset, after accounting for endowment and structural effects of capital constraints using Eq. (5). Results show that an egalitarian institutional environment enhances the potential of women's educational investments for bridging gender-yield disparities, whereas the role of equitable credit access is more emphasized in a conservative society.

We further exploit the SEM to integrate interactions between capital constraints and farming strategies, and predict the total effects (the sum of direct and indirect paths) of each intervention. Defaulting average male-headed households as the still reference, we narrow a certain proportion of gender-resource gaps by targeted investments towards female-headed households (Fig. 10b). A first aspect to highlight is that facilitating gender-equitable access to marketing channels exerts the most significant effects, which potentially offsets 30 % of genderproductivity gaps and enhances yields on women-dominated farms by 2.5 %. Simultaneously, investments in women's education have the potential to narrow 16.5 % of the productivity gap, translating to a noteworthy 1.4 % increase in productivity of women-dominated farms. A combination of four designed interventions synergistically bridge 49 % of gender-productivity gaps.

Women's market inclusions are still constrained by gendered responsibilities, including a frequently identified restriction imposed by domestic caregiving on women's mobility and time-use agency (Pierotti et al., 2022; Berniell et al., 2021). Table 8 presents the effects of intra-household norms on gendered market participation and validates the caregiving penalty. Results suggest that old-age caring burden increases 3 % of gender disparities in market participation, which dominantly restricts women's profitable opportunities. Complementary evidence from a nationwide survey conducted in 2014 shows that 86.2 % of the left-behind rural women are restricted by domestic caregiving in their mobility, time-use agency, and economic opportunities (China Women's News, 2023).

## 6. Discussion and policy implications

## 6.1. Too light to touch: empowering women or inducing gendered responses?

While previous body of literature are insufficient in providing quantitative or qualitative evidence regarding the domains of empowerment, debates around where women-targeted interventions would yield the highest returns have driven a series of policy experiments and discussions rethinking the interactive roles of capital constraints and social norms (Doss, 2018; Akram-Lodhi, 2024). Nevertheless, recent studies examining the impact of a gender-sensitive food system intervention in Burkina Faso mainly reveal an undesirable consequence (Heckert et al., 2023; Leight et al., 2022; Eissler et al., 2020). Karamba and Winters'(2015) confirm a slight effect of input subsidy program in Malawi on bridging gender gaps, highlighting troubles in designing well-targeted agricultural policies. Programs neglecting gender-specific resources, constraints, and responsibilities always yield gendered responses and are too light to touch rural women's welfare.

By integrating farmer's resource-contingent responses under different institutional contexts as mediated pathways, our study sheds light on the debates from a perspective of “to learn how to learn"10. We uncover the determinative role of inequitable education attainment and market opportunities, and the moderating role of local gender institutions, underlying gender-productivity gaps in agriculture. Tracing through the recursive mechanisms, women's lower investments, yields, and efficiency are rooted in an inequality in knowledge and motivations, further trapped by intra-household roles and biased norms.

Direct “give-away" schemes such as microfinance and input subsidies show inefficiency in providing disproportionate support to women, due to their constrained ability to participate in and benefit from agricultural projects (Quisumbing et al., 2015). Financial and information literacy, practical farm knowledge and experience, market accessibility and profit-orientation, as well as skewed resource allocation patterns prevent women from acquiring equal productive returns from direct asset transfers as men. Domestic responsibilities embedded within intra-household norms further restrict their autonomy to participate and grasp economic opportunities (Eissler et al., 2020).

Agricultural policies that empower women to gain more equitable access to fundamental resources and development opportunities, rather than directly offer funds or input materials, may exert more profound influences on boosting farm women's productivity. To catch up with commercialized agri-food systems, studies investigating market inclusion unveil that strengthening women's linkages to markets is a crucial pathway for empowering women in agriculture (Raghunathan et al., 2021; Gupta et al., 2017). Nevertheless, a number of marginalized farmers are still excluded from agricultural markets, including women groups with limited control over assets and suppressed intra-household bargains. Market-based policies should be reframed with concerns over women's capacity to generate a marketable surplus (Heckert et al. 2023; Djurfeldt et al., 2018), the focal point of which lies in addressing limitations posed by start-up capitals. Beyond ensuring women's practical control over implements and financial capitals, the role of horizontal participatory networks, involving farmer cooperatives and women's groups, is emphasized in strengthening their collective solidarity, active mobility, and power for independent marketing (Quisumbing et al., 2015).

Another aspect of transformative mechanisms corresponds to the deeply ingrained patriarchal norms at both household and regional level. On the one hand, local society with patriarchal or egalitarian norms reshape gender-specific returns to resource endowments, which raises the bar of equitably benefiting from agricultural interventions. Heckert et al.'s (2023) research on market-focused interventions emphasizes that institution-targeted empowerment tools should be parallelly integrated into policy portfolios, regarding a reconfiguration of farm culture and power relations. Farmers in crop sales are confronted with transaction costs of information searching, contract negotiation, monitoring, and implementation in the imperfect market environment (Coase, 1937). Within the confines of restrictive gender norms, disenfranchised rural women frequently encounter hurdles inhibiting their independence to partake in markets and broaden their sales channels. Even in instances where women engage in crop markets, they may attain diminished returns arising from prevailing gender disparities in negotiating power, bargaining dynamics, risk mitigation, and their ability to access and assimilate market information (Eissler et al., 2020), which raises a knotty question for the policy design.

On the other hand, intra-household norms around division of reproductive labor aggravate women's labor constraints and time poverty (Akram-Lodhi, 2024). Extant studies have unveiled the penalty of caregiving burdens on women's subsequent growth, persisting as a formidable barrier to bridging gender inequality (Sinha et al., 2024). Stressed by triple-responsibility as mothers, wives and daughters (law-in-daughters), women lack agency to engage in educational trainings, market activities, and off-farm works, as well as leisure time for self-care, resulting in poorer physical health and economic well-being. These challenges demand restructuring deep-rooted aspirations about gender roles and a transformative societal influence (Zhang et al., 2024).

Yet a knowledge gap remains in understanding China-specific mechanisms and how they diverge across countries. Land tenure regimes in Africa and South Asia suffer from persistent conflicts between statutory laws and customary practices, which incur strong resistance to women's registration of land ownership and inheritance rights (Agarwal, 1994; Boone, 2014). The intersection of ethnicity and gender reshapes practices of land institutions and patterns of land inequality. This contextual barrier is consistent with an emphasized role of reconfiguring asymmetric land ownership in bridging gender-productivity gaps in SSA and South Asia. While in China, women may face fragile, vulnerable tenure rights in marital-based land disputes (i.e., cross-village marriage or divorce) with limited legal safeguards. A gender-biased land certification program further threatens their tenure insecurity. These pitfalls reshape women's land rental and management.

A common ground of research specific to China and South Asia is the stressed inequality in knowledge, experience, and motivation, highlighting the role of education- and market-focused investments rather than direct “give-away" subsidies or transfers. China's central policies have proposed to build up women's capacity for sustainable livelihoods by a series of “quality enhancement" schemes aimed at cultivating welleducated professional women farmers (State Council, 2023). Additionally, a widespread cultivation of “New Agricultural Operators" dominates the agrarian transition in rural China, including scaled-up family farms, farmer cooperatives, and dragon-head agribusinesses (Zhang, 2019). Specialized farmer cooperatives are operated democratically as economic mutual-help organizations, which can build up social support networks for isolated female members in production and marketing. Agribusinesses develop vertical integration forms with smallholders (Yan and Chen, 2013) and effectively decrease the transaction costs borne by peasants in their quest for information retrieval and contract negotiation. Another concurrent trend is the increasing female-head scaled-up farms or female-led cooperatives and agribusinesses. The role of women leaders—in both business and political domains—in transforming discriminatory norms and decentering power relations may be the breach for a more gender-inclusive market environment.

A key difference between Africa and Asia regarding how patriarchal norms reshape gender inequality in agriculture is the source of time poverty. While social norms around reproductive labor exert crosscountry influences, in the context of China and South Asia, the restrictive role of unpaid old-age caring burden is further emphasized beyond the child penalty, particularly those living with the father-in-law under the legacy of traditional “filial piety" culture. With notable demographic aging in rural China, the ratio of the elderly over 65 years old has reached 17.72 % (90.35 million) (NBS, 2021). Recent massive labor out-migration has led to smaller-sized rural households (Su et al., 2022; Zhao et al., 2016), imposing heavier caregiving pressure on the left-behind rural women. In a local society with sticky practices of patriarchal gender roles and limited discourse in governance for women, the elderly care penalty on rural women's livelihoods is further compounded by a lack of progress in community-based formal caring services, as persistent societal expectations impose triple roles upon women as dutiful daughters (law-in-daughters), mothers, and wives. Freeing up these time and labor constraints demands a transition of traditional family care towards socialized care based on community institutions and services.

## 7. Conclusion

This article delves into the intricate mechanisms shaping gender inequality in agriculture. Our findings indicate that farming strategies contingent upon natural, financial, physical, and human capitals are the mainstay in the causal chain of gendered agricultural productivity that explains 62–82 % of total effects, while the unexplained direct effects still account for 20–30 %. Gendered patterns of land rental and abandonment, mechanization, and other input utilization can be interpreted in the function of gender inequality in capital constraints and biased institutional contexts.

Through path analysis, we find that women's restricted educational attainment and access to marketing channels are root causes undermining their knowledge, capacity and motivation to intensify cultivation and enhance farm productivity. The policy simulations suggest that investing in gender-equitable market connections have the potential to reduce 30 % of gender-productivity gap. Another aspect to highlight is that institutional environment exerts moderating roles in the mechanism. Within the reach of conservative gender beliefs and attitudes, returns to women's resource endowments are substantially undermined. We also find that the domestic burden of unpaid old-age care imposes a disproportionate constraint on women's participation in profitable marketing. By visualizing structural inequality, our study concludes that imperfections in input markets exert a pervasive influence, with farms exhibiting lower productivity more susceptible to unobserved institutional constraints. Further improvement in gender-sensitive interventions should establish an integrative framework taking intricacies between resource reallocation and institutional background into consideration.

One inherent methodological limitation is that assuming uncorrelated residual terms and their independence from predictors, for an unbiased SEM estimates, could be too strong in practice. While we have included a rich set of confounders to address omitted-variable concerns, it's still difficult to rule out endogeneity biases sourced from reverse causality in resource-contingent decision-makings. Considering this limitation, we sharpen the research focus of the SEM approach on uncovering interactive influencing pathways—complementary to conclusions drawn on fixed-effects estimates—while enriching our theoretical foundation and hypotheses based on the Sustainable Livelihood Framework.
