# Sustainable Intensification and Large-scale Operation of Cultivated Land Use at the Farmers’ Scale: A Case Study of Shandong Province, China

LI Li¹ 2, LYU Xiao3, 4, ZHANG Anlu1, NIU Shandong3, PENG Wenlong³

(1. School of Public Administration, Huazhong Agricultural University, Wuhan 430070, China; 2. Department of Geography, University of Zurich, Zurich 8057, Switzerland; 3. School of Humanities and Law, Northeastern University, Shenyang 110169, China; 4. School of Geography and Tourism, Qufu Normal University, Rizhao 276800, China)

Abstract: Sustainable intensification of cultivated land use (SICLU) and large-scale operations (LSO) are widely acknowledged strategies for enhancing agricultural performance. However, the existing literature has faced challenges in precisely defining SICLU and constructing comprehensive indicators, which has hindered the exploration of factors influencing LSO within the SICLU framework. To address this gap, we integrated self-efficacy theory into the design of an index framework for evaluating SICLU. We subsequently employed econometric models to analyze the significant factors that impact LSO. Our findings reveal that SICLU can be divided into four key dimensions: intensive management, efficient output, resource conservation, and ecological environment optimization. Furthermore it is crucial to incorporate belief-based cognitive factors into the index system, as farmers' understanding of fertilizer and pesticide application significantly influences their willingness to engage in LSO. Moreover, we identify grain market turnover as the most influential factor in promoting LSO, with single-factor contribution rates reaching 70.9% for cultivated land transfer willingness and 62.5% for the total planting areas. Interestingly, unlike irrigation and agricultural machinery inputs, increased labor inputs correspond to larger planting areas for farmers. This trend may be attributed to reduced labor availability because of rural labor migration, whereas the reduction in irrigation and agricultural input is contingent on innovations in production practices and the transfer of cultivated land management rights. Importantly, SICLU dynamically influences LSO, with each index related to SICLU having an optimal range that fosters LSO. These insights offer valuable guidance for policymakers, emphasizing farmers as their central focus, with the adjustment of input and output factors as a means to achieve LSO as the ultimate goal. In conclusion, we propose research avenues for further enriching the SICLU framework to ensure that it aligns with the specific characteristics of regional agricultural development

## 1 Introduction

The sustainable intensification of cultivated land use (SICLU) and large-scale operations (LSO) are approaches aimed at enhancing the efficiency of cultivated land utilization (Paul and Nehring, 2005; Wu et al., 2020). SICLU emphasizes qualitative development, whereas LSO focuses on quantitative expansion (Tang et al., 2015). Harmonious integration has the potential to contribute significantly to global human well-being through sustainable agricultural development (Tilman et al., 2002). First, SICLU has emerged as a solution to address environmental issues resulting from excessive intensification, such as declining irrigation water quality, heavy metal pollution of the soil, and ecological imbalances (Vanbergen et al., 2013; Zhao et al., 2015). Drawing from research conducted by Lyu et al. (2021; 2022a), Lu (2020), and Niu et al. (2021; 2022) on SICLU within the specific agricultural conditions of China, we strongly advocate that SICLU offers a sustainable approach to land resource utilization. It can adjust, align, and optimize the flow of various materials on land, reduce dependence on external investments in land (Petersen and Snapp, 2015), enhance land output efficiency (Pandey et al., 2002; Zhang et al., 2017), and expand ecosystem resilience (Jepson et al., 2014; Mdee et al., 2019). Alternatively, LSO pertains to the scale of production factors allocated by economic entities, primarily involving the allocation of physical assets such as labor, capital, and land, which can be quantified. The transfer of land management rights is a pivotal mechanism for promoting LSO. However, unlike the international standard of 6.67 ha or even 66.67 ha per household, it appears that a few hectares or even less of cultivated land per household is a pragmatic choice for future land management in China (Lin, 2017). The allocation of production materials relevant to SICLU, such as labor, machinery, fertilizers, and pesticides, has also influenced farmers’willingness to engage and the extent of their engagement in LSO in China (Zhang and Qian, 2008; Zhang et al., 2015; Nie et al., 2019).

Previous studies have predominantly focused on either SICLU or LSO in isolation, often overlooking their interrelationships. However, the key to optimizing the allocation of agricultural resources lies in understanding the factors influencing LSO in the context of SICLU. As highlighted by Xie et al. (2021), SICLU can be conceptualized as a process involving the flow of materials and energy necessary to sustain land productivity and land use. The fundamental principle underlying SICLU is to elevate agricultural output levels within the constraints of maximum environmental carrying capacity. Notably, farmers’ motivation to pursue LSO is directly linked to the prospect of increased net income through large-scale agriculture (Wen et al.,

2017). Given that achieving a higher output necessitates harmonious coordination of various agricultural resources, we can establish a connection between SICLU and LSO by examining the input and output dynamics of these materials.

Sun and He (2019) claimed that an intensive agricultural management model has been established in most provinces of central and eastern China. The critical question that arises is, how will SICLU impact China's LSO? Given the limited in-depth research on SICLU to date (Lyu et al., 2022b), our study aims to address the following questions. First, we aimed to delineate the essential core of SICLU from four dimensions: management models, output capacity, resource utilization, and external effects to clarify the starting point of this research. Second, we constructed a framework of indicators for SICLU from the perspectives of farmers’ subjective perceptions and practical behaviors with the goal of uncovering the interconnection between SICLU and LSO from an input-output' standpoint. Third, we used survey data from farmers in Shandong Province, a major agricultural region in China, and employed econometric models to identify significant factors and assess their contributions towards facilitating LSO with the aim of identifying the key factors that harmonize SI-CLU with LSO. Ultimately, the findings of our research will offer valuable scientific support for sustainable development efforts, including the harmonization of SICLU and LSO, the optimization of land-use patterns, and improvements in agricultural production management. Importantly, these insights allow us to share China's agricultural experience of achieving simultaneous environmental capacity enhancement and production performance improvement with the global community.

## 2 Theoretical Framework

Psychological science plays a pivotal role in understanding the mechanisms of individuals’ decision making, and considering farmers’ cognitive processes has greatly enriched research on ecological behaviors (Robert et al., 2016; Dessart et al., 2019). The intricate and dynamic system of agricultural intensification was primarily influenced by the cognitive beliefs and specific knowledge held by various stakeholder groups (Micha et al., 2020). For instance, Lynne and Rola (1988) proposed that farmers with stronger inclinations towards protection were more likely to engage in soil conservation practices in Florida's Panhandle. Similarly, Vignola et al. (2010) discovered that farmers’ soil conservation actions were closely tied to their understanding of soil erosion, encompassing their beliefs, risk perceptions, and the value they placed on it in Costa Rica. Singh et al. (2016) comprehensively examined farmers'decisionmaking mechanisms and revealed that social cognition, including perceived adaptive capacity and belief in their ability to implement adaptive actions, served as the primary driving force behind farmers’ adoption of climate adaptation behaviors. However, current agricultural intervention measures and policies related to SICLU often failed to fully consider farmers’ cognitive aspects, such as their perceptions, beliefs, priorities, and interests (Aravindakshan et al., 2021). Neglecting these essential elements and disregarding farmers’ preferences could lead to unsustainable LSO practices (Han, 2020). Hence, it is imperative to incorporate famers’ beliefbased cognitive factors into the SICLU index system.

Uphoff (2014) highlighted that employing systems thinking can be more effective in addressing interactions both between and within subsystems. Subsequently, we could distill the core essence of SICLU that suited China's agricultural context drawing from the following four dimensions (Niu et al., 2018; Lyu et al., 2022a). 1) Intensive management. This dimension entails optimizing material input allocation and driving a shift in management practices, process control, and monitoring of various input factors to achieve efficient resource utilization. 2) Efficient output. Building on the effective inputs of water, fertilizers, pesticides, machinery, and labor, this dimension focuses on producing a seamlessly integrated, high-quality output. It not only emphasizes quantity, it considers the importance of quality in production. 3) Resource conservation. With guaranteed output as the baseline, this dimension views reduced material inputs during the production process as a means of optimizing and enhancing intensive management practices. 4) Enhancement of the ecological environment. This dimension seeks to mitigate negative environmental impacts throughout all stages of agricultural production, thereby diminishing the harm caused by chemical products such as fertilizers and pesticides to the agricultural ecosystem. Enhancing the ecological environment aims to bolster the resilience and restorative capacity of agricultural ecosystems. In parallel, we evaluated LSO by examining factors such as farmers’ willingness to transfer land and the area of cultivated land they manage.

We centered our analysis on farmers as the primary stakeholders and constructed an SICLU index with a focus on self-efficacy and status-quo bias, as illustrated in Fig. 1. Self-efficacy is cultivated and developed based on individuals’ confidence in their ability to process and evaluate various forms of information. Essentially, it reflects people's beliefs in whether they possess the skills and capabilities necessary to complete a task or activity (Bandura, 2018). Self-efficacy is a cognitive factor that drives individuals to engage in specific behaviors or make particular choices (Guo and Jiang, 2008). Under the influence of self-efficacy, farmers develop a psychological state referred to as SICLU in terms of belief and cognition'. This means that when farming households perceived that they have achieved SICLU, they become more confident in producing agricultural products of higher quality and value. Their expectations of future income also rose significantly. This heightened confidence and optimism could substantially boost farmers willingness to transfer their cultivated land and encourage them to expand their planting areas. In contrast, status quo bias denotes the inclination of individuals to stick with existing choices when making decisions (Samuelson and Zeckhauser, 1988). This bias is closely tied to loss aversion because people tend to be more sensitive to potential losses when the status quo changes (Dong, 2006). Consequently, farming households exhibit a SICLU in implement intensity'. This means that smallholders have path dependence on the current land use pattern, and any abrupt shift in these practices entails additional production costs, such as information gathering, mode reconstruction, and feedback monitoring. As a result, farmers tend to favor maintaining their current modes of cultivated land use (Zhang et al. 2016). In this context, if their existing practices align more closely with the principles of SICLU, farmers will benefit from path dependence, leading to a higher income. This, in turn, enhances the likelihood of transfer and the expansion of cultivated land.

## 3 Materials and Methods

## 3.1 Study area

Shandong Province, China was selected as the case for several reasons. First, Shandong Province stands out as a prominent grain-producing region in China, greatly benefiting from the favorable conditions offered by the expansive North China Plain, which enables large-scale agricultural operations. Second, Shandong Province is an economically developed region in eastern China. The pressing need to ensure the food supply within this prosperous province makes it a crucial focus for SICLU. We adopted a systematic approach to gain a comprehensive understanding of Shandong Province. Following the guiding principles of balanced geographical representation and varying levels of economic development, we carefully selected the study areas. These areas include Wulian County in Rizhao City, situated along the eastern coastal region; Kenli District in Dongying City, located within the fertile northern Yellow River Delta Plain; and Dongping County in Tai'an City, located within the mountainous terrain of Shandong Province (Fig. 2). Our decision was further substantiated by an analysis of statistical yearbooks from these three cities, which allowed us to compile data on agricultural development in Wulian, Kenli, and Dongping in 2016 (Table 1). These data reveal that the study area possesses favorable conditions and a strong foundation for agricultural development. Notably, disparities in rural employment figures, total grain crop acreage, and fertilizer usage across the three locations explain these variations. Importantly, these variations aligned with our study area selection criteria, ensuring that our chosen areas collectively represented both the common and unique features of Shandong Province's agricultural landscape.

## 3.3.2 Boosted Regression Tree

While linear regression can help explore the initial impact of SICLU on LSO, it fails to capture the dynamic response relationship between significant influencing factors and the dependent variables. Relying solely on Pvalues is insufficient, as it can not offer a precise index interval and threshold for enhancing the willingness to transfer cultivated land and expand the cultivation area. To address this limitation, we introduced the Boosted Regression Tree (BRT) in R (https://www.r-project.org/), which is a two-class machine learning algorithm that combines the strengths of the regression tree method and boosting technique (Elith et al., 2008). The output from the BRT yields the following insights. 1) When the relative influence value of an independent variable on the dependent variable is greater than zero, it indicates a positive stimulating effect of the independent variable on the dependent variable. 2) Conversely, when the relative influence value of the independent variable on the dependent variable is less than zero, it signifies a negative inhibitory effect of the independent variable on the dependent variable. 3) When the relative influence of the independent variable on the dependent variable is zero, there is no discernible correlation between the independent and dependent variables at that particular point in time.

## 3.4 Indicators and variables

In the contemporary context, there were no universally established indicators for the measurement and evaluation of SICLU, as highlighted in studies by Westbury et al. (2011) and Barnes and Thomson (2014). Previous research has suggested that comprehensive indicators were more compelling than single indicators when assessing SICLU (Irwin, et al., 2018). For instance, Firbank et al. (2013) measured SICLU at the farm scale by considering agricultural environmental pressures and economic outputs. Gómez-Limón and Riesgo (2009) developed SICLU evaluation indices based on economic, social, and environmental sustainability. Musumba et al. (2017) identified five essential dimensions for achieving SICLU: productivity, economic viability, human welfare, environmental impact, and societal implications. Building on this, Reich et al. (2021) further argued that a more comprehensive evaluation of agricultural performance could be achieved by expanding the monitoring scope, geographical regions, and range of indicators used to assess SICLU.

The critical task of identifying suitable material input and output indicators has now taken center stage in the selection of core independent variables. These variables serve a dual purpose: they not only provide insights into farmers’ SICLU based on their subjective perceptions and objective actions, but also exert an influence on LSO. Studies conducted by Gadanakis et al. (2015) and Kyalo Willy et al. (2019) have previously explored the impact of different inputs—such as agricultural machinery, labor, fertilizer, water, and seeds—on corn (Zea mays) and rice (Oryza sativa) production. In our study, we extended this perspective by incorporating farmers' psychological factors into the analysis. The specific indicators employed were as follows.

(1) The inclusion of farmers’ psychological indicators in SICLU index represents an interdisciplinary endeavor, as noted by Irwin et al. (2018). Considering the Chinese zero-growth program for chemical fertilizers and pesticides, their application to rice, wheat (Triticum aestivum), and corn cultivation has surpassed 40% (MARA, 2021). Additionally, farmers possessed a pragmatic understanding of the quantities, frequencies, and impacts of their use. Furthermore, with a specific focus on production materials pertaining to fertilizers and pesticides, we devised four subjective indicators related to policy, quality, quantity, and pollution perceptions.

(2) The incorporation of the four SICLU dimensions effectively elucidates the objective conditions governing farmers' practices. Within the input-output indicator framework of production materials, the following insights emerge. First, intensive management underscores the efficient utilization of resources. Previous studies have shown that investments in improved crop varieties such as corn, wheat, sorghum (Sorghum bicolor) (Spielman et al., 2010; Epper et al., 2020) and agricultural machinery (Baudron et al., 2015; Mottaleb et al., 2016; Aryal et al., 2021) significantly promoted SICLU. We considered seed input per ha and machinery input per ha as degree indicators of material utilization. The former may increase because improved seeds typically command higher prices than ordinary seeds. The per ha cost increase of the latter signifies improved mechanization, resulting in enhanced agricultural economic benefits and, consequently, a positive impact on LSO. Efficient output places equal emphasis on quantity and quality. The crop yield per unit was the most commonly used indicator in SICLU literature, as suggested by (Smith et al., 2017). Agricultural income is another essential economic indicator that characterizes the degree of SICLU Notably, within our study area, grains serve a dual purpose: they satisfy family and livestock consumption needs and the surplus is sold in the market. To capture the actual output of SICLU, while avoiding deviations stemming from neglecting ration losses, we selected grain output per unit area and total grain transaction turnover (as opposed to turnover per unit) as indicators. Third, resource conservation centers minimize material input waste during production. Key indicators representing the degree of SICLU include agricultural labor (Dahlin and Rusinamhodzi, 2019) and irrigation (Pandey et al., 2002; Kropp et al., 2019). Hence, we employed the total labor input and irrigation input per ha as indicators of factor-saving degrees. When labor input was minimized, farmers were likely to adopt higher levels of mechanization, and reduced irrigation input indicates improved water use efficiency, both of which correlate with an increased willingness to transfer land and expand planting areas. Finally, ecological environment enhancement entailed internalizing the negative effects of fertilizers and pesticides. A valuable approach was to reduce the input levels of these chemicals once the output margins are reached (Sheahan et al., 2013; Holden, 2018; Kiwia et al., 2019). Accordingly, we speculated that increasing fertilizer and pesticide inputs per unit area could stimulate farmers to engage in LSO; however, beyond a certain threshold, excessive input may have a detrimental effect on LSO

In addition to the core independent variables, we included additional variables related to household factors, such as the age of the household head and the average education level of family members, as well as variables associated with the state of village land markets, including the stability of land property rights and the presence of new agricultural entities. The rationale for selecting these variables was well documented in prior research, as highlighted by Mungai et al. (2016). Descriptive statistics for these variables are presented in Table 2.

## 4 Results

## 4.1 Descriptive statistical results

Previous research has lacked a strict basis for dividing cultivated land scales, leading to significant disparities in division results. We grouped farmers based on the statistical characteristics of the cultivated land total planting areas in the surveyed region's sample. In this survey, the cumulative frequency of farmers with cultivated land operation scales of 0.47 ha or less was 57.82%. Drawing on the experiences of farmers in the surveyed area regarding scale operations, we used 0.47 ha as the threshold to categorize farmers into small-scale farmers’ and large-scale farmers’ (Table 3).

(1) Transfer willingness (y1): Within the core variable group, the proportions of farmers in the no willingness to transfer cultivated land’ and willingness to transfer cultivated land’groups who were aware of the zero-growth plan for chemical fertilizers and pesticides (x1) were 14.85% and 29.23%, respectively (Table 3). In both groups, farmers believed that pesticides had a relatively significant impact on grain quality (x12). The proportions of samples in the no willingness to transfer cultivated land’group and the willingness to transfer cultivated land’group who believed that excessive use of chemical fertilizers (x13) and pesticides could pollute the environment (x14) are 40.61% and 44.62%, respectively; for chemical fertilizers and pesticides are 75.55% and 76.92%, respectively. Additionally, the average cost of seeds per unit area (x15) in the willingness to transfer land’group is 740.85 yuan RMB/ha, which is 0.98 yuan RMB /ha lower than the no willingness to transfer land’ group. The willingness to transfer land’group also exhibits higher agricultural machinery input per unit area (x16) (1508.13 yuan RMB/ha), total labor input (x19) (18.23 d), irrigation input per unit area (x20) (304.92 yuan RMB/ha), chemical fertilizer input per unit area (x21) (2584.77 yuan RMB/ha), and pesticide input per unit area (x22) (491.24 yuan RMB). Except for chemical fertilizers and pesticides, all input values in the no willingness to transfer land’ group were lower than those in the willingness to transfer land’ group. Furthermore, the willingness to transfer land'’ group had higher grain yield per unit area (x17) and grain market turnover amounts (x18) compared to the ‘no willingness to transfer land' group.

(2) Planting areas (y2): Within the core variable group, both the cultivated land planting areas less than 0.47 ha’group and the “cultivated land planting areas greater than or equal to 0.47 ha"group had relatively low proportions of farmers who were aware of the zerogrowth plan for chemical fertilizers and pesticides (x11) (Table 3), standing at 11.72% and 20.24%, respectively. However, a larger planting areas was associated with a better understanding of the policy. The proportions of respondents in both groups that believed that pesticides negatively affected grain quality (x12) were 78.56% and 73.81%, respectively. In the smaller-scale group, there was a lower proportion of farmers who believed that the excessive use of chemical fertilizers and pesticides (x13), with differences of 21.38% compared to the larger-scale group. Respondents believed that pesticide could pollute environment (x14), had the highest proportion in both groups. Statistically, the sum of the means for cost of seeds per unit area (x15), agricultural machinery input per unit area (x16), irrigation input per unit area (x20), chemical fertilizer input per unit area (x21), and pesticide input per unit area (x22) in the cultivated land operation area less than 0.47 ha’group is 5616.82 yuan RMB/ha, which is 138.76 yuan RMB /ha higher than that in the larger-scale planting areas'group. However, the smaller-scale group invested an average of 0.06 fewer days in labor (x19). In terms of output, the smallerscale group achieved a unit area grain yield (x17) of 6690.81 kg/ha, whereas the larger-scale group achieved 6585.76 kg/ha. The grain market turnover amounts (x18) varied significantly between the two groups because of differences in planting areas.

Indirect benefits include reduced material costs, enhanced ecological value of agricultural products, and sustainability of cultivated-land ecosystems, which collectively resulted in higher benefits for farmers, as suggested by Perry and Davenport (2020).

Blindly pursuing higher grain yields may hinder LSO. A closer examination revealed that farmers invest intensive materials in smaller cultivated land plots to achieve high yields per unit area. Maintaining a small cultivation area is a prerequisite for achieving a high yield per unit area. Survey data supports this observation, revealing that when the cultivated land area was less than 0.47 ha, farmers achieved a grain output per unit area that surpassed those with an area of 0.47 ha or more by 5.08 kg (Table 4). This suggests that LSO not only emphasizes high grain production but also pursues the goal of reducing input costs (such as chemical fertilizers, pesticides, seeds, and agricultural machinery) (Jin, 2016). This cost-reduction concept aligned with the overarching objective of improving economic benefits (Xu et al. 2011).

Agricultural input indicators such as labor, irrigation, and machinery inputs had a significant impact on largescale farming. The migration of agricultural labor was likely to result in insufficient land-use intensification and a loss of scale efficiency. In China, the phenomenon of male out-migration for work, while women and the elderly engage in farming’ is quite common (Leng et al., 2020). The decreasing availability of labor poses a significant threat to the grain supply (Morse et al., 2002; Zhang et al., 2021). Assuming a constant irrigation water price, irrigation inputs are largely influenced by seasonal water scarcity and are therefore not discussed extensively. Instead, we focus on agricultural machinery investment. Liu et al. (2018) argued that agricultural mechanization investment promoted LSO through an investment lock-in effect and cost savings. However, this conclusion lacked empirical validation and overlooked material constraints in terms of labor substitution. Despite Shandong Province's relatively large per capita cultivated land area compared to other provinces in China, the distribution of land plots would never reach the concentration and scale observed in American farms (Zheng and Ying, 2017). Viable approaches to facilitate LSO include accelerating the transfer of agricultural land management rights and developing outsourcing services for agricultural machinery. The former consolidated the rights of spatially fragmented plots and represented a key measure employed by the Chinese government to modernize agriculture in recent years. Agricultural machinery services include self-purchasing and social outsourcing. The smallholder business model led Chinese farmers to favor professional social services over costly machinery purchases (Luo, 2020). However, the current landscape of agricultural mechanization services in China has not yet been fully developed (Cao and Wang 2018). Typically, farmers with agricultural machinery, rather than professional organizations, outsource services to small-scale farmers (Diao et al., 2014; Cai and Liu, 2018), and the high cost of machinery input inhibited farmers from expanding their cultivation areas.

## 5.2 The formation mechanism of SICLU and LSO The ecological and environmental challenges stemming

from excessive intensification have sparked academic discussions regarding SICLU. The central question revolved around the potential existence of a Jevons Paradox’ (Ceddia et al., 2013) between sustainability' and ‘intensification'. Some scholars in agreement with this perspective argued that farmers’rationality often drived them to increase their use of chemical fertilizers and pesticides. In the short term, the results in the improved productivity of cultivated land, leading to more frequent utilization (Ceddia et al., 2013). Additionally, programs such as Zambia's corn subsidy have hindered the positive aspects of sustainable intensification by reducing the substitution between crop planting and rotational fallow (Morgan et al., 2019). Conversely, opposing viewpoints suggested that the relationship between sustainability’and intensification' has evolved from tension to harmony (DeClerck et al., 2016). For example, advancements in agricultural mechanization in Ethiopia have made it possible to achieve simultaneously sustainability and intensification (Mutyasira et al., 2018). Brazil has also successfully implemented a national strategy to halt deforestation in the Amazon while increasing livestock production through the adoption of intensive technologies (De Oliveira Silva et al., 2018). Our research operated under the premise that sustainability and intensification could complement each other, with one of our primary objectives being to uncover the essence of SICLU and elucidate its formation mechanism (Jules et al., 2018). We posited that agricultural production factors were pivotal in defining SICLU (Barnes and Thomson, 2014). We believed it was essential to incorporate farmers’ self-efficacy into the SICLU index, which should be constructed based on belief cognition and implementation intensity while addressing aspects related to the management, input, and spillover effects of agricultural materials.

LSO is an approach the Chinese government is recommended to take to address the crucial question of ‘who will farm in the future?’ This concept is often associated with the development of new agricultural management entities. LSO can be achieved through the transfer of land management rights, offering a practical solution for overcoming the constraints associated with natural resource endowments. In our study, we employed farmers’ willingness to transfer and planting areas of cultivated land to characterize LSO. We investigated the significant factors influencing farmers’LSO in the context of SICLU. Our findings revealed variations in the impact of SICLU factors on farmers’ transfer willingness and planting areas. Notably, self-efficacy indicators had a substantial impact on farmers' planting areas, indicating that Chinese farmers' LSO are primarily influenced by their past planting practices, with less regard to whether these practices align with SICLU criteria. While this may boost short-term agricultural productivity, it raises concerns about the unsustainable long-term use of cultivated land. Furthermore, it is important to emphasize that the formation of LSO is not solely determined by any single SICLU indicator, but rather results from the interplay of various indicators. These include an output index related to grain market turnover and input indices covering agricultural machinery, labor, and agricultural irrigation. The maximization of output or minimization of input directly dictates the extent of LSO.

## 5.3 Performance evaluation for SICLU and LSO

A sustainable intensive production mode could enhance the crop yield per unit of energy consumption and minimize agricultural material waste (Sánchez-Escobar et al., 2018; Mwalupaso et al., 2019). The implementation of SICLU in different locations offered compelling evidence for evaluating its effectiveness. For instance, Keil et al. (2016) observed that adopting zero tillage practices in India resulted in a remarkable increase of 498 kg/ha in wheat yield, accompanied by reduced energy and irrigation input costs. Furthermore, Bais-Moleman et al. (2019) contended that SICLU has the potential to curtail greenhouse gas emissions and enhance land use efficiency. This can be achieved by altering human dietary habits, integrating food waste into livestock diets, transitioning from monoculture cropping to crop rotation, and incorporating crop residue into soil. Moreover, the Montpellier Report proposed a global agricultural assessment system based on SICLU principles (Jules et al., 2018). In summary, compared with traditional methods, SICLU has demonstrated its ability to enhance agricultural performance without exceeding the ecological environment's carrying capacity threshold. It contributed to increased food production while reducing unnecessary inputs (Pandey et al., 2002; Pretty et al., 2011; Dahlin and Rusinamhodzi, 2019; Srivastava et al., 2019), enriched agricultural biodiversity (Bunting et al., 2015; Xie et al., 2019), and bolstered the land's sustainable supply capacity (Islam et al., 2019; Oldfield et al., 2019). Consequently, SICLU exhibited substantial potential and favorable outcomes across economic, social, and ecological dimensions (Gatto et al., 2020).

LSO originated from scale payments practiced by agricultural entities. However, policy deviations could impede the achievement of expected goals, such as conserving input materials and realizing economies of scale. The use of agricultural machinery for planting and harvesting, along with the employment of a large workforce and the procurement of agricultural materials at low prices, may not sufficiently offset the rising agricultural production costs associated with the price premium of large-scale agricultural land transfer’ and the ratchet effect of wages of employees under the Lewis turning point' (Shang and Zhu, 2015). Additionally, these practices could lead to a misalignment between LSO and the government's policy objectives of ensuring food security and a stable supply of important agricultural products. Given that, we contended that the input and output of agricultural production materials not only determined the attainment of LSO but also significantly influenced its effectiveness. Furthermore, the impact of objective factors on the scale performance can not be disregarded. In particular, land fragmentation in China maintained the overall return on grain production at a constant level. This implied that pursuing LSO solely to increase grain production may not be advisable (Xu et al., 2011). Policymakers in countries with agricultural development levels similar to China should consider this aspect.

We devised an indicator system to evaluate SICLU in Shandong Province of China, focusing on the input and output of production materials, as per our research objectives. However, it was important to recognize that this indicator system may only capture certain aspects of the conditions within the surveyed area. As the study of SICLU continues to evolve, researchers must continually define a set of standardized indicators or delineate their applicability boundaries (Tittonell, 2014). This implied an ongoing process of adding new indicators capable of measuring SICLU (Järnberg et al., 2018). For instance, researchers should comprehensively assess whether SICLU indicators encompass factors such as resource endowments in the study area (degree of cultivated land fragmentation and water facility development), farmers’ agricultural production experience (management techniques and farming methods), ecological spillover effects (soil physical and chemical properties and biodiversity), and other relevant elements, depending on the specific research objectives. Furthermore, the achievement of LSO did not hinge on any single policy or technological innovation, nor can it be attributed solely to changes in management or institutional factors (Lee et al., 2006). Additionally, it was important to note that each indicator may not have a straightforward, monotonic effect on farmers’ willingness to transfer or expand their cultivated land. Therefore, a critical area for future research is to adjust and balance the optimal input ranges for various agricultural materials to maximize the benefits of LSO.

## 6 Conclusions and Policy Implications

## 6.1 Conclusions

In this study, we focused on farmers as the primary subjects. First, we delineated the core essence of SICLU and incorporated farmers’ psychological cognitive factors into the framework of SICLU. Using OLS and BRT models, we approached the research from the perspective of the input-output’ of agricultural production materials to identify the significant factors that dynamically influenced farmers’ land transfer and planting areas. The following conclusions were drawn.

(1) SICLU assessment could be conducted in four dimensions: operational intensification, output efficiency, resource conservation, and ecological environment optimization. These four dimensions reflected the core of SICLU. However, as research on SICLU continues to advance, the range of applicable evaluation indicators was expected to expand. Farmers were the primary agents of production and operation, and it was crucial to incorporate their beliefs and cognitive factors into the SICLU indicator system. Farmers’ understanding of policies related to fertilizer and pesticide application significantly affected their willingness to engage in LSO, which, from a self-efficacy perspective, could influence the transformation of agricultural modernization.

(2) Reducing input costs and increasing output benefits were both goals for farmers. The impact of the inputs and outputs of agricultural materias on LSO is a dynamic balancing process. While grain market turnover was the most significant driver of LSO, the pursuit of higher yields may lead to a significant increase in labor, irrigation, and machinery inputs. These input indicators offseted the positive contribution of the output indicators to LSO. Therefore, reducing the hindrance of agricultural input factors to LSO was beneficial for achieving harmony between SICLU and LSO.

## 6.2 Policy implications

(1) Incorporating farmers’self-efficacy into the SICLU index. To ensure accuracy, policymakers should interpret SICLU-related policies, such as straw return-tofield and a zero-growth plan for fertilizer and pesticide usage, and construct an SICLU index based on criteria such as intensive management, efficient output, resource conservation, and ecological environment optimization. The goal was to translate farmers'strong SICLU awareness into high self-efficacy.

(2) Optimizing the allocation of agricultural production materials to promote LSO. Policymakers should consider the direction of influence and factor contributions of various input-output indices on LSO. This may involved developing employment outsourcing services and relying on the third-party service agencies to offer farmers cost-effective seeding, irrigation, harvesting, and other comprehensive services. This also meant replacing gender-based labor divisions within families with socialized divisions in agricultural production. Furthermore, policymakers should enhance agricultural subsidies to stabilize the prices of essential production materials and strengthen farmers' capacity to implement SICLU. Expanding the scope of agricultural subsidies and raising subsidy levels for technology adoption are essential.

(3) Improving various agricultural support policies. Given that the primary driving force of LSO came from the income generated through scale payments, policymakers should establish reasonable minimum grain purchase prices to prevent low grain prices from harming farmers. Additionally, they should expand the scope of agricultural subsidies, increase subsidy standards for technology adoption, and promote the modernization of agricultural production. Policymakers needed to recognize the dynamic impact of various SICLU-related factors on LSO. To successfully promote LSO, a combination of policies, including land and fiscal policies, was essential. This holistic approach was necessary to prevent individual variable changes from deviating from the original objectives of LSO.

(4) Accelerating the transfer of cultivated land management rights. Encouraging farmers to transfer cultivated land to address the limitations posed by land fragmentation on LSO. Concentrating land rights could offset the negative effects of spatially dispersed land ownership and resolve the issue of “who will farm?" in the context of labor migration.
