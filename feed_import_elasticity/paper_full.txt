Abstract
China's feed-grain imports are a critical margin of food-security policy because they connect global grain markets with domestic livestock, poultry, dairy, and aquaculture supply chains. When corn import costs , trade-policy frictions alter source-country access, or geopolitical events interrupt grain shipments, policymakers need to know whether import demand reallocates smoothly across alternative energy feed grains. This paper estimates a conditional import-demand system for corn, barley, sorghum, cassava, and oats using Chinese  customs data for . The preferred specification is a Shonkwilercorrected QUAIDS model with quality-adjusted unit-value prices and a import Bartik controlfunction instrument for endogenous import expenditure. The Bewley LRB test rejects AIDS in favor of QUAIDS, and the Bartik instrument is strong in the expenditure first stage. Delta-method inference shows that all expenditure elasticities and all own-price elasticities in the selected QUAIDS model are statistically significant. Barley has the highest expenditure elasticity, oats the lowest, and all ownHicksian elasticities are negative. Corn-price scenario simulations show that alternative feed grains provide a real but limited substitution margin: compensated substitution is positive, but fixed-budget responses are muted by the higher cost of corn. The evidence supports trade-risk diversification through both product substitution and source-country diversification while cautioning against treating any single substitute or origin as a frictionless replacement for imported corn.

1. Introduction
Feed grains occupy a strategic position in China's food system. Imported corn, barley, sorghum, cassava, and oats are not mainly final household foods. They are intermediate inputs used by feed mills livestock and poultry farms trading firms starch and alcohol processors and downstream meat, egg, dairy, and aquaculture supply chains.  policy question is not simply whether China imports more or less feed grain in aggregate. It is whether the import portfolio can reallocate across alternative energy feed grains when a product becomes costly, quota constrained, or difficult to source.
This question has become more salient as trade-policy shocks have repeatedly affected the landed cost and availability of key feed ingredients. Corn imports are linked to quota administration and domestic grain-policy objectives. Barley has experienced major policy disruptions and subsequent reopening. Sorghum is technologically relevant in feed formulations but highly exposed to source-country concentration and bilateral frictions. Cassava is tied to tropical supply chains and has both feed and industrial uses. Oats is a smaller and more specialized product. These differences imply that measured substitution elasticities combine feedformulation substitutability with logistics, contracting, port access, and policy feasibility.
The aggregate import-demand literature provides the first building block. In the imperfect-substitutes framework, import demand is modeled as a function of domestic activity and relative import prices; the choice of domestic activity variable and dynamic specification affects the estimated elasticities (Goldstein and Khan, 1985; Carone, 1996; Senhadji, 1998). Evidence for China shows that aggregate import elasticities are sensitive to whether the scale variable is GDP, GDP net of exports, national cash flow, or disaggregated expenditure components (Tang, 2003). Monthly evidence further shows that exchange-rate conditions and global risk perception shape China's imports (Wang and Lee, 2012). More recent work indicates that aggregate price elasticities can vary with population and preference composition (Fukumoto, 2025). These studies motivate careful interpretation of import-demand elasticities, but their aggregate focus cannot identify substitution within the feed-grain import portfolio.
A second  source-differentiated agricultural import demand. The Armington framework establishes that traded products can be differentiated by place of production (Armington, 1969). Demandsystem applications relax the restrictive assumptions embedded in simple ArmingtonCES specifications by estimating budgetshare systems for differentiated imports (Deaton and Muellbauer, 1980; Banks et al., 1997). Studies of Japanese meat imports, Korean meat demand, and Korean wine imports show that aggregation, block separability, and source restrictions can strongly affect estimated elasticities (Yang and Koo, 1994; Henneberry and Hwang, 2007; Lee et al., 2009). This paper follows that logic at the product level: corn, barley, sorghum, cassava, and oats are related energy feed ingredients, but they are not identical inputs.
A third strand emphasizes price transmission and policy wedges. Export-demand elasticities for major U.S. crops depend on how world prices are transmitted into importingcountry prices (Reimer et al., 2012). Import-demand evidence for U.S. potash shows that a strategic agricultural input with few substitutes can have highly inelastic import demand, trade restrictions mainly  costs rather than induce quick substitution (Annan and Reimer, 2025). Two-stage Armington estimates for Japan's meat imports further show that tariffrate quotas, non-ad-valorem policy regimes, and exchange-rate instruments can substantially change substitution estimates (Nakano and Nishimura, 2024). These findings are directly relevant because Chinese customs unit values combine landed cost, quality, shipment scale, origin composition, contract timing, and policy wedges.
A fourth literature addresses censored demand, unit values, and theory regularity. Zero purchases or zero imports are common in micro and trade data; participation corrections allow the extensive margin to enter share equations rather than being discarded (Shonkwiler and Yen, 1999). Unit values contain quality and composition effects as well as prices (Deaton, 1988; Cox and Wohlgenant, 1986). djusted-quality prices are widely used in fooddemand systems when actual prices are unavailable (Han and Chen, 2016). Flexible demand systems can violate regularity when data are sparse and price measures are noisy; local curvature restrictions are essential for credible welfare and substitution interpretation (Diewert and Wales, 1987; Ryan and Wales, 1998).
This paper these strands in the context of China's feed-grain import portfolio. We use Chinese customs records aggregated to a panel for . The five-product panel contains 4,340  cells; 1,745 are positive imports and 2,595 are zeros. Zero rates are high even at quarterly frequency: 0.550 for corn, 0.509 for barley, 0.577 for sorghum, 0.688 for cassava, and 0.666 for oats. Intermittent importing is therefore a structural feature of the market rather than a nuisance to be removed.
The empirical strategy follows the logic of recent  articles that first motivate the policy problem, then identify the data and measurement challenges, then present the econometric strategy and diagnostics before the results. The preferred specification is a Shonkwilercorrected QUAIDS model estimated with adjusted prices and a import Bartik control function. AIDS is estimated as the nested linearEngel benchmark, and model selection is based on the Bewley likelihoodratio statistic. The selected model directly imposes , homogeneity, and symmetry; own-price signs and Slutsky local negativity are checked before any price elasticity is interpreted.
The contribution is threefold. First, the paper estimates conditional import-demand elasticities for a strategic intermediateinput portfolio rather than for aggregate imports or final consumer foods. Second, it treats zero imports, unit-value prices, and endogenous import expenditure as central identification issues rather than as data-cleaning inconveniences. Third, it distinguishes between statistically theory-regular substitution and policy-ready causal claims. The selected QUAIDS model passes the local theory gate, but the estimates remain conditional import-allocation elasticities; they do not by themselves identify total feed demand, the domesticimport split, or welfare effects.
The results support a cautious view of import substitution. All five products are normal goods within the conditional import budget, but the budget response is heterogeneous. Barley is above unit elastic with respect to importbudget expansion, corn is close to unit elastic, sorghum and cassava are below unit elastic, and oats is the least budget responsive. All own-price elasticities are negative and the Slutsky matrix satisfies local negativity. Cross-price and Morishima elasticities indicate that substitution exists, but the estimated magnitudes are moderate. Scenario calculations show that a corn-price shock  only limited fixed-budget reallocation, trade-risk management should combine product substitution with source-country diversification and inventory policy.

Section 4 reports the empirical results. Section 5 discusses mechanisms and policy interpretation. Section 6 concludes with limitations and directions for future work.
2. Data and price measurement
The empirical analysis uses Chinese customs import records aggregated to the  level. The product system contains five imported energy feed grains: corn, barley, sorghum, cassava, and oats. This boundary is important because the estimates describe substitution within an imported energy feed-grain portfolio, not substitution across all imported and domestic feed raw materials.

3.1 Conditional import-allocation object
Let r index provinces, t index quarters, and i index products in I = {corn, sorghum, cassava, oats, barley}. Let qᵢᵣₜ denote import quantity, pᵢᵣₜ the quality-adjusted landed unit value, and vᵢᵣₜ = pᵢᵣₜqᵢᵣₜ the import value. The conditional import budget for the five-product system is
Xᵣₜ = ∑_{i∈I} pᵢᵣₜ qᵢᵣₜ = ∑_{i∈I} vᵢᵣₜ.        (1)
and the budget share of product i is
wᵢᵣₜ = pᵢᵣₜ qᵢᵣₜ / Xᵣₜ.        (2)
The system is conditional on Xᵣₜ > 0. he elasticities describe how the observed imported feed-grain portfolio reallocates across products when prices or the conditional import budget change. They are not unconditional income elasticities, not total feed-demand elasticities, and not estimates of the domesticimport substitution margin.
3.2 AIDS and QUAIDS share equations

where Zᵣₜ contains observed feed-demand, domestic-price, logistics, and source-network controls. The real expenditure term is
yᵣₜ = ln Xᵣₜ − ln a(pᵣₜ)        (4)

AIDS is nested in QUAIDS under the restriction λᵢ = 0 for every product. The quadratic term is important in this application because provinces with larger import budgets need not scale all feed grains proportionally. Instead, they may shift toward products with deeper import markets, more stable logistics, or more favorable policy access.

3.6 FIML estimation and model selection

If R(θ) is the n by m residual matrix for the m estimated equations and Sigma(theta) = R(θ)'R(θ)/n, the concentrated objective is proportional to
obj(θ) = n ln |Σ(θ)|.        (15)

4.2 Expenditure elasticities
Table 4 reports expenditure elasticities from AIDS and the selected QUAIDS model, with deltamethod standard errors in parentheses. All QUAIDS expenditure elasticities are positive and statistically significant at the 1% level, all five products are normal goods within the conditional import budget. The ranking is economically informative: oats has the lowest elasticity at 0.555, cassava and sorghum are below unit, corn is close to unit, and barley has the highest elasticity at 1.308.
The QUAIDS model sharpens the differences that AIDS smooths over. Relative to AIDS, QUAIDS lowers the elasticities of sorghum, cassava, and oats while increasing the elasticity of barley. This is exactly the kind of nonlinear Engel effect that motivates QUAIDS. As the five-product import budget expands, importers do not scale all products proportionally. The portfolio tilts toward barley and, to a lesser extent, maintains corn near proportionality, while oats remains a specialized component whose expansion is limited by market size and use.
Barley's elasticity of 1.308 should not be read as a final-food luxury interpretation. In this intermediate-input context it means that barley's budget share  more than proportionally when provinces expand the imported energy-feed budget. This is consistent with  relatively large mean share and broad use as an energy substitute when import conditions are favorable. Oats' elasticity of 0.555 is the opposite: it expands with the budget but less than proportionally, consistent with a small specialized market.

4.6 Source-country exposure and trade-risk diversification
Product substitution does not  trade risk. Each substitute has a source-country network. Recent customs-based reporting indicates that Brazil has rapidly surpassed the United States as China's leading corn supplier, after China approved Brazilian corn purchases in 2022, and that U.S. corn sales to China fell sharply in 2024 (Chu and Beijing Newsroom, 2025). The same  notes that U.S. sorghum remains important but faces competition from Australia and Argentina and from cheaper Brazilian corn (Chu and Beijing Newsroom, 2025). Barley illustrates the policynetwork dimension: China imposed an 80.5% tariff on Australian barley in 2020, trade was redirected toward other markets, and the trade was expected to swing back after the dispute was resolved; the episode shows why a low-cost supplier can still be a high-risk supplier if access is policycontingent (Al Khawaldeh, 2023). China's broader food-security policy also emphasizes self-sufficiency and supply stability  a complex international environment (Chu and Cash, 2025).

5. Discussion
The selected QUAIDSSY model changes the interpretation of China's feed-grain import substitution. Because the model is estimated with , homogeneity, and symmetry imposed and because it passes local Slutsky negativity, the elasticities are more suitable for policy interpretation than unrestricted flexible diagnostics. The new deltamethod standard errors also show that the main expenditure and own-price effects are statistically precise.
The expenditure elasticities reveal an additional mechanism. All five products are statistically significant normal goods within the conditional import budget, but their elasticities are not equal. This heterogeneity is precisely why QUAIDS is selected over AIDS: the import portfolio displays nonlinear budget allocation rather than simple proportional scaling.
The own-price elasticities provide a consistent but conservative picture. All own Hicksian elasticities are negative and the Slutsky matrix is locally negative semidefinite. important because the price responses can be interpreted as demand-system elasticities rather than artifacts of noisy unit values. , the cross-price effects are small. he selected model supports a policy language of limited substitution.  does not support claims that importers can offset a corn shock through large, frictionless shifts into other imported grains within the same quarter.
The contrast between Marshallian and Hicksian scenarios is also informative. The Marshallian response is relevant when importers face a fixed nominal budget: higher corn prices absorb the budget and limit purchases of other products. The Hicksian response is relevant when the objective is to isolate substitution after compensating for the higher cost of corn. The gap between the two responses is the budget-squeeze channel. This channel explains why barley can be a strong compensated substitute but still  a slightly negative fixed-budget response when the corn price : the higher corn cost crowds out available nominal expenditure unless the import budget expands.
Finally, the source-country discussion shows that product diversification and origin diversification are . Barley and cassava can help buffer corn shocks, but only if their own supplier networks are kept open and diversified. Sorghum should be managed as a contingent substitute because its U.S.-centered trade channel is exposed to tariff and diplomatic shocks. Oats should remain a niche component rather than a crisis-scale substitute. These findings are consistent with a portfolio approach to food-security policy: resilience comes from maintaining multiple products, multiple origins, and flexible logistics, not from relying on a single estimated cross-price elasticity.
6. Policy implications and conclusions
The counterfactual results translate directly into policy priorities. If imported corn becomes 10% more expensive, the selected QUAIDS model predicts  9.8%  in corn quantity under a fixed conditional budget. This paper has several limitations. The estimates are conditional on positive five-product import expenditure and therefore do not estimate total feed demand, the domesticimport split, or welfare. Unit values are adjusted for shipment scale and source composition, but they may still contain unobserved quality and policy wedges. Province-level customs records may capture importer or declaration  rather than final feed use. The preferred instrument is strong in the first stage, but it is identified, so overidentifying restrictions cannot be tested. A full food-security counterfactual would need to combine this within-import allocation layer with a total importbudget model, domestic feed-ingredient substitution, and source-country supply shocks.
The central conclusion is that China's imported feed-grain portfolio provides a real but limited substitution margin. The selected QUAIDSSY model is consistent and shows that all products have negative own-price effects and positive compensated substitution relationships. However, the scenario analysis demonstrates that corn-price shocks generate modest reallocation unless budgets and source networks also . should therefore treat barley and cassava as the most operational near-term buffers, sorghum as a contingent substitute exposed to diplomatic and source-country risk, and oats as a small specialized component. The most robust strategy is not a single substitute but a managed portfolio of products, origins, contracts, and reserves.
