# China's dietary transition to 2050 reshapes global agricultural markets and environmental footprints

**Shengnan Huang¹, Xinru Han¹\***

¹ Institute of Agricultural Economics and Development, Chinese Academy of Agricultural Sciences, Beijing 100081, China

\* Corresponding author: hanxinru@caas.cn

*Manuscript prepared for submission to Nature Food (Analysis). Main text ≈ 4,300 words; Methods ≈ 3,100 words; 4 main figures, 1 main table, 4 Extended Data figures; Supplementary Information attached.*

---

## Abstract

What 1.4 billion Chinese consumers eat by 2050 will be decided within China, but its consequences will not stay there. Using a sequentially linked China agricultural-sector model and multi-region world agricultural market model, we quantify how three dietary pathways—preference-driven upgrading, convergence to a composite healthy-diet benchmark, and a moderate half-way transition—propagate through domestic nutrition, multi-dimensional environmental footprints and world markets. A healthy-diet transition cuts world pork prices by 56% and soybean prices by 35%, contracts Brazilian and Argentine soybean production by 12–14%, and reduces harvested cropland outside China by 21.7 Mha, while world milk-powder prices rise 20% against the trend. Globally, it lowers agricultural emissions by 0.50 Gt CO₂e (−9.3%), with 77–96% of carbon, water, nitrogen and land physical reductions occurring outside China. In the moderate scenario as currently constructed, half the demand-growth adjustment is associated with 55–65% of the PTS-to-HDS changes, making gradual, policy-guided dietary change a globally consequential mitigation pathway rather than proving a general concavity result.

---

## Main

China's food system is undergoing a profound dietary transition. Rising incomes, urbanisation and population ageing have shifted consumption away from grain-centred diets towards more animal-source foods, edible oils, fruit and aquatic products¹⁻³. Because fat carries more than twice the energy per gram of carbohydrate, this structural shift has raised the fat share of dietary energy even as total energy intake plateaued, and it has moved China's food-security challenge from calorie adequacy towards diet quality, non-communicable disease risk and environmental pressure⁴⁻⁷. Food security in China can therefore no longer be assessed through staple-grain sufficiency alone; it requires a food-systems lens that connects demand, nutrition, resources, greenhouse-gas (GHG) emissions and governance⁸,⁹.

A rich literature has established that dietary change is central to keeping food systems within environmental limits¹⁰⁻¹⁴, and China-focused work has shown that healthier Chinese diets can—but do not automatically—deliver environmental co-benefits¹⁵⁻¹⁷, that no observed Chinese dietary pattern simultaneously satisfies nutrition, environment and cost objectives¹⁸, and that bundled food-system measures generate larger co-benefits than isolated interventions¹⁹,²⁰. Yet three gaps remain. First, almost all assessments treat China's dietary future as a domestic question, comparing national diets against national resource and emission accounts. This misses the defining feature of China's food economy: it is the world's largest importer of soybeans, meat and dairy, so Chinese demand shifts are transmitted through world markets to land use and emissions in Brazil, the Americas, Oceania and beyond—a telecoupling channel²¹,²² that existing diet-scenario studies for China have not quantified with an explicit multi-region market model. Second, environmental assessments typically use a single indicator (usually GHG) with domestic production coefficients, creating an unacknowledged inconsistency when the underlying model trades internationally: emissions "saved" in China may simply be displaced abroad, or conversely, savings abroad may go uncounted. Third, the literature compares current diets with recommended endpoints while paying little attention to the transition path between them, even though real-world dietary change is gradual, path-dependent and constrained by preferences, affordability and supply²³—and even though ageing and population decline are reshaping how many "standard consumers" China must feed²⁴,²⁵.

Here we address all three gaps in a single framework. We soft-link the China Agricultural Sector Model (CASM v2.2.7), a recursive-dynamic partial-equilibrium model of China's agricultural sector with four household groups and out-of-home consumption, to CASM-World, a 13-region, 31-commodity world agricultural market model with PEATSim-type structure, and to an internationally sourced, fully cited coefficient library for carbon, blue water, reactive nitrogen and land. The link is a one-way demand-shock transmission experiment: China dietary paths are translated into CASM-World demand-preference shifters, and world prices are not iterated back into the domestic CASM solution. Against a baseline (BS) of fixed food preferences, we compare three dietary pathways to 2050: a preference-driven transition (PTS) in which income elasticities evolve with per-capita GDP following cross-country regularities²⁶,²⁷; a healthy-diet scenario (HDS) converging to a composite benchmark built from five dietary-guideline systems, including the Dietary Guidelines for Chinese Residents (2022) and the EAT-Lancet reference diet¹⁴; and a moderate transition scenario (MTS) whose per-capita demand growth is the arithmetic mean of PTS and HDS. Each pathway is crossed with six demographic variants (population high/medium/low, urbanisation high/low, and an ageing adjustment using adult-equivalent consumers), yielding 19 scenarios (Table 1). All model code, scenario inputs, coefficients and validation reports are open source.

Three results structure the paper. First, dietary pathway—not urbanisation, and more than population size—dominates China's food demand and nutrition-related outcomes, and a healthy-diet transition rebalances the macronutrient structure that preference-driven upgrading further distorts. Second, at fixed technology the diet-composition effect on China's own farm-gate footprint is modest (±10%), which is precisely why single-country accounting misleads: the large effects occur abroad. A Chinese healthy-diet transition removes China from world pork and feed-grain import markets, cuts world pork prices by 56% and soybean prices by 35%, shrinks soybean production in Brazil and Argentina by 12–14%, and reduces harvested cropland outside China by 21.7 Mha—while dairy, the one food group that expands towards guideline levels, pushes world whole-milk-powder prices up 20%. Third, accounting for trade-induced production adjustment worldwide, the global net effect of China's healthy-diet transition is −0.50 Gt CO₂e (−9.3% of world agricultural farm-gate emissions), −89 km³ blue water, −10.4 Mt reactive nitrogen and −556 Mha of land occupation equivalent, with 77–96% of the physical reductions occurring outside China; a moderate transition is associated with 55–65% of these changes at half the dietary-growth adjustment. China's dietary transition is thus not a domestic nutrition policy question but a global resource reallocation—and a demand-side mitigation lever of gigatonne scale.

## Results

### Three dietary futures restructure China's food demand and nutrition

Figure 1 summarises the modelling framework and the three dietary pathways against the composite healthy-diet benchmark band. The pathways diverge sharply in what China eats, far more than any demographic variant changes how much (Fig. 1; Supplementary Tables 2–4). Under the baseline, per-capita rice and wheat consumption decline to 96.6 kg and 55.8 kg by 2050 while every animal-source food rises; under PTS (scenario A1), dynamic income elasticities amplify this upgrading, lifting pork to 42.4 kg, dairy to 57.5 kg and edible oils to 25.6 kg. HDS (B1) inverts the animal-food mix rather than merely shrinking it: pork falls to 11.0 kg (−71% versus BS 2050) and beef to 2.5 kg, while dairy more than doubles to 102.1 kg, aquatic products rise to 60.4 kg (+110%) and poultry to 32.6 kg. MTS (C1) tracks the midpoint—pork 21.8 kg, dairy 76.7 kg, aquatic products 43.4 kg. By contrast, high versus low urbanisation moves most per-capita items by less than ~2.5%, and population variants rescale aggregate demand by roughly ±7% without changing its composition; the ageing adjustment (adult-equivalent population of 1.270 billion in 2050 versus 1.259 billion head-count) lowers aggregate demand across all food groups by 3–7% but never reorders the pathways (Supplementary Tables 5–7).

The nutrition consequences are first-order (Fig. 2). Dietary energy under BS rises from 2,958 kcal per capita per day in 2024 to 3,036 kcal in 2050, and PTS pushes it to 3,091 kcal with the fat share of energy reaching 41.7% (BS 2050: 39.7%)—far above the WHO 30% ceiling and continuing the historical trajectory in which fat displaced carbohydrate even as total energy stabilised³,⁴. HDS lowers energy intake to 2,258 kcal and restores macronutrient balance (fat 33.0%, carbohydrate 48.1%, protein 17.4% of energy), while keeping protein intake high (98.1 g per day). MTS reaches 2,572 kcal with a 36.7% fat share. Benchmarked against dietary guidelines, red-meat intake in 2050 is 143 g per day under BS and 157 g under PTS—roughly 3–4 times the healthy range—versus 80 g under MTS and 40 g under HDS, which enters the guideline band; dairy, conversely, remains below the Chinese guideline floor of 300 g per day even under HDS (280 g), identifying dairy as the binding "guideline gap" of China's dietary transition (Supplementary Table 8). Market-driven upgrading and health-oriented transition are therefore not points on one continuum but qualitatively different futures: the former deepens the fat-heavy structure, the latter simultaneously moderates energy, rebalances macronutrients and re-sorts protein sources from red meat towards dairy, aquatic foods and poultry.

### Multi-dimensional footprints in China: composition effects are real but modest

We next translate each scenario into four environmental footprints—GHG, blue water, reactive nitrogen and land—using a fully cited international coefficient library, with FAOSTAT farm-gate emission intensities as the main carbon accounting and Poore & Nemecek cradle-to-retail life-cycle coefficients as an upper-bound sensitivity (boundaries are never blended; Methods). Holding emission and resource coefficients fixed at 2023 values to isolate the pure diet-composition-plus-trade signal, China's 2050 farm-gate consumption carbon footprint is 652 Mt CO₂e under BS, and the 19-scenario spread runs from −7.4% (MTS with low population) to +10.0% (PTS with high urbanisation); blue water spans −1.7% to +3.4% around 338 km³, reactive nitrogen −1.2% to +3.9% around 46.2 Mt N, and diet-related land occupation −9.9% to +10.9% around 1,206 Mha (Fig. 2; Supplementary Table 9). Three features stand out. First, the C group (MTS) lowers every footprint simultaneously—the only pathway family with across-the-board co-benefits—whereas HDS's large dairy and aquatic expansion partly offsets its red-meat cuts in the water and nitrogen dimensions. Second, when scenario-specific supply-side technology improvement is included, as in conventional single-country accounting, China's total emissions fall to 451 Mt under HDS (−31% versus BS) and 502 Mt under MTS; comparing the two accountings shows that roughly two-thirds of that headline reduction is technology, not diet, a decomposition that single-metric studies conflate. Third, under the cradle-to-retail life-cycle boundary the same diets imply a consumer footprint an order of magnitude larger (BS: 7,607 Mt CO₂e), underscoring that most diet-driven emissions embodied in Chinese consumption arise beyond the farm gate and beyond China's borders (Extended Data Fig. 2).

This is the pivotal observation: with production technology fixed, diet composition moves China's own farm-gate footprint by only ±10%, because domestic production adjusts sluggishly and the demand shock exits through trade. The question "what does China's dietary transition do to the environment?" therefore cannot be answered inside China.

### Global transmission: China's diet shift repriced and re-mapped world agriculture

Feeding the three dietary pathways into CASM-World reveals transmission at a scale rarely associated with a single country's consumer behaviour (Fig. 3). Under HDS in 2050, relative to BS, China's food pork demand is 53 Mt lower; feed maize demand falls by 41 Mt, feed soymeal by 22 Mt and soybean crushing by 10 Mt. China's net imports fall by 40.6 Mt for pork (from 11.7 Mt net imports to net-export pressure), 35.9 Mt for maize, 7.4 Mt for soybeans and 14.3 Mt for soymeal, and China switches from the world's largest rice and wheat importer to a net exporter. World prices respond accordingly: pork −56%, soybean oil −48%, beef −40%, soybeans −35%, maize −33%, wheat −32%, rice −37% relative to BS 2050. Under PTS the signs reverse—pork +9%, soymeal +6%, maize +4%—so the *choice* of pathway spans a two-regime world food-price future.

The one systematic exception is dairy. Because dairy is the only major food group that expands towards guideline levels under HDS, and China's dairy self-sufficiency falls from 0.75 (BS) to 0.37 (HDS), world whole-milk-powder prices rise 20% (skim-milk powder +6%) against an otherwise deflationary landscape. Export dividends flow mainly to the European Union and the United States (whole-milk-powder production +33% and +41%), with New Zealand's milk-powder exports up 20% (2.43 to 2.91 Mt) while Australasian butter and cheese lines are crowded out as limited raw milk is reallocated to the booming powder segment.

Supply-side geography reorganises around these prices. Brazilian soybean production contracts 11.7% (186.3 to 164.5 Mt) and Argentine production 14.0%; US soybean output falls 7.1% and US pork production—the region most exposed to Chinese import demand—falls 46%. Beef output in Brazil, Argentina and Australia declines 21–22%, while EU beef production rises 6.8% as collapsing world feed prices lower its costs. Summing across regions, harvested cropland outside China falls by 21.7 Mha under HDS (−2.3%)—an area comparable to the entire arable land of the United Kingdom—concentrated in the rest-of-world aggregate (−9.1 Mha), India (−4.3 Mha) and South America (−3.6 Mha); PTS instead expands ex-China cropland by 0.5 Mha (Fig. 3). China's dietary choices thus operate as a global land-sparing (or land-claiming) policy administered through prices, with the supply-side carbon and biodiversity stakes located almost entirely on other continents²¹,²².

### The global net effect: a China policy with a predominantly ex-China footprint

Applying region-resolved footprint coefficients to the trade-adjusted production of all 13 world regions yields an internally consistent global net-effect account—the answer to the trade–emissions inconsistency that afflicts single-country diet studies (Fig. 4). Against a 2050 world farm-gate baseline of 5.31 Gt CO₂e, 1,244 km³ blue water, 144 Mt reactive nitrogen and 1,047 Mha harvested cropland, China's healthy-diet transition delivers global net changes of −495 Mt CO₂e (−9.3%), −89 km³ blue water (−7.1%), −10.4 Mt reactive nitrogen (−7.2%), −556 Mha of land occupation including pasture (−8.8%) and −22.7 Mha of harvested cropland (−2.2%). The distribution is the headline: 91% of the carbon saving, 77% of the water and nitrogen savings, 88% of the land-occupation release and 96% of the cropland release occur *outside* China. China's own farm-gate account improves only modestly (−45 Mt CO₂e) precisely because the demand reduction is transmitted abroad through trade; a domestic-only account would miss nine-tenths of the climate benefit—or, under PTS, miss a global *increase* of +64 Mt CO₂e and +82 Mha of land occupation that continued dietary upgrading imposes on the rest of the world.

These magnitudes place China's dietary transition among the largest single demand-side mitigation levers available anywhere: 0.50 Gt CO₂e per year is comparable to the total annual emissions of a G7 economy's agriculture sector several times over, achieved without any assumed technology change, land-use-change credit or carbon pricing. Per capita, the healthy-diet pathway is also the only one that brings China's farm-gate food emissions below an equal-per-capita share of the EAT-Lancet food-system carbon boundary (Supplementary Table 10).

### A moderate transition secures most of the modelled change at half the adjustment

Full convergence to the healthy-diet benchmark by 2050 demands historically unprecedented behavioural change—pork consumption falling by two-thirds, dairy more than doubling. The moderate transition scenario, whose per-capita demand growth is by construction the arithmetic mean of PTS and HDS, tests how much of the modelled PTS-to-HDS change appears under a smaller adjustment (Fig. 4). MTS attains 62% of the PTS-to-HDS reduction in dietary energy, 58% of the fat-share correction and 66% of the red-meat reduction; 70% of the world pork-price transmission and 65% of the soybean-price transmission; 65% of the global net carbon saving (−299 Mt CO₂e, −5.6%) and 58% of the ex-China cropland reduction (−12.4 Mha). Across 21 nutrition-proxy, market and environmental indicators the median realisation is 65%, and most indicators fall in the 55–65% band (Supplementary Table 11). Because MTS is defined by growth-rate interpolation, this pattern should be interpreted as a property of the scenario construction and model response together, not as standalone evidence that transition returns are generally concave. Testing endpoint-level and log-level interpolation is a priority for the sensitivity analysis before making a stronger economic claim. For policy, the result still converts the healthy-diet benchmark from an all-or-nothing target into a direction in which smaller consumption adjustments can deliver substantial modelled changes.

## Discussion

This study reframes China's dietary transition from a domestic nutrition question into a global resource-reallocation problem, and three implications follow.

First, dietary transition pathways should be evaluated with trade-consistent, multi-region accounting. Our fixed-coefficient decomposition shows that diet composition alone shifts China's farm-gate footprint by only about ±10%, while the same demand shifts move world prices by tens of percent and relocate 12–14% of South American soybean production. Studies that apply domestic emission factors to a trading economy either overstate domestic reductions (by crediting China with reductions that in fact occur through technology assumptions) or understate global physical reductions (by ignoring the 77–96% of changes that occur abroad). The global net-effect framework used here—domestic footprint change plus trade-induced production adjustment in all partner regions, under one coefficient boundary—resolves this inconsistency and, we suggest, should become standard practice for national diet-scenario studies, complementing global integrated assessments²⁸ with country-led market detail. The telecoupling is not incidental: it is the main event²¹,²².

Second, the direction of transition matters more than its existence, and dairy is the strategic exception that proves it. Preference-driven upgrading (PTS) is itself a transition—diets diversify, staples decline—yet it raises the fat share of energy to 42%, keeps red meat at 3–4 times guideline levels, tightens world meat and feed markets, and adds 64 Mt CO₂e to global emissions. Health-oriented transition reverses all of these—except for dairy, where China's guideline gap (280 g per day achieved versus a 300 g floor even under full convergence) makes China structurally import-dependent (self-sufficiency 0.37 under HDS) and lifts world milk-powder prices 20% against the deflationary trend. Anticipating this counter-cyclical dairy channel matters for exporters (the EU and US, not Australasia, capture most of the powder gain in our simulations) and for China's own nutrition policy, which cannot deliver its dairy recommendation without either a much larger domestic dairy sector or deliberately secured import channels. More broadly, the aquatic-product expansion under HDS (60 kg per capita) presses on a supply channel—aquaculture feed—that our world model does not yet cover, flagging fish–feed linkages as the next transmission channel to quantify.

Third, a moderate pathway remains policy-relevant, but the mechanism must be stated cautiously. The 55–65% realisation at half the growth-rate adjustment reflects both the interpolation rule and the market response, so it should not yet be read as proof of declining marginal transition cost. Dietary habits are culturally embedded and policy levers are soft²³,²⁹; a benchmark that demands halving pork consumption within a generation invites non-compliance, whereas a moderate pathway framed as "direction plus pace" captures substantial modelled changes while leaving supply chains, farm livelihoods and trade partners more time to adjust. This finding extends the bundled-measures logic of recent China food-system assessments¹⁹,²⁰ to the temporal dimension, and it speaks to the observation that national food-system transformation pathways are typically strongest on production and weakest on consumption²⁹: a moderate demand-side pathway is the component such national strategies most tractably add. The distributional corollary is that China's dietary policy is de facto foreign agricultural policy, especially for Brazilian soy growers, US hog producers and dairy exporters, arguing for embedding dietary-transition scenarios in agricultural trade diplomacy and for anticipatory adjustment support in exporter regions.

Limitations bound these conclusions. CASM covers primary and first-stage processed commodities: highly processed foods, sweets and sugar-sweetened beverages—rising with income and urbanisation—are represented only through the sugar and oil channels, so our nutrition-proxy results likely understate the unhealthy tail of PTS; the world model omits sheep meat, eggs, aquatic products and horticulture, so the HDS aquatic channel's global effects are uncounted. The sign of this omitted-channel bias is ambiguous because aquaculture can substitute for livestock while also increasing fishmeal, soymeal and grain-feed demand. Footprint coefficients are held at 2023 levels to isolate the diet signal—supply-side technical change would scale, but not redirect, the results—and the Mekonnen–Hoekstra water coefficients (1996–2005) predate two decades of Chinese irrigation-efficiency gains, so China's blue-water level should be read as an upper bound. Behavioural change is imposed through preference shifters rather than derived from a policy module, and results are national averages without income-group or regional disaggregation. None of these caveats touches the central asymmetry: the environmental consequences of what China eats in 2050 will materialise mostly outside China, and a moderate, policy-guided transition captures a large share of the modelled PTS-to-HDS change.

## Methods

### The China Agricultural Sector Model (CASM)

CASM v2.2.7 is a recursive-dynamic, multi-market partial-equilibrium model of China's agricultural sector maintained at the Institute of Agricultural Economics and Development, Chinese Academy of Agricultural Sciences. It covers 37 crop, livestock and processed commodities and solves, for each year from the 2023/2024 base to 2050, a mixed-complementarity equilibrium in which domestic supply, demand components and trade clear every commodity market simultaneously. On the supply side, crop production is the product of sown area and yield, with area responding to lagged own- and cross-prices through partial-adjustment acreage equations and yield following exogenous technical progress with price responsiveness; livestock output responds to output prices and feed costs through supply elasticities and explicit feed-conversion linkages to the feed-grain and protein-meal markets. On the demand side, total domestic use is disaggregated into food, feed, processing, crushing, seed, waste and stock demand. Food demand is modelled for four household groups—urban and rural, each split into at-home and away-from-home consumption—so that out-of-home consumption, which rises with income and urbanisation and biases household-survey-based assessments when omitted¹⁷, is endogenous. The base-year food-consumption database reconciles National Bureau of Statistics household-survey quantities with supply-utilisation accounts, allocating the survey-unrecorded residual to away-from-home and processing uses; the reconciliation procedure, elasticity matrices and all base-year balances are published in the open model repository (Code availability). Domestic and border prices are linked through ad valorem tariff-and-margin wedges and the exchange rate, and net trade adjusts endogenously to clear each market, which is how demand-side scenario shocks are transmitted to (and from) world markets.

The full algebraic specification, parameter files and a cell-level validation against the original GAMS implementation are provided in the repository. The Python re-implementation used here reproduces the GAMS solution to machine precision (median relative deviation ≤ 1.4 × 10⁻¹⁶ across ~1,076 compared cells per scenario for all 19 scenarios; Extended Data Fig. 4). Two legacy artefacts of the original implementation were repaired and documented rather than inherited: (i) nutrition outputs for BS/PTS in one legacy workbook derived from an outdated energy-coefficient table (for example, BS 2050 dietary energy of 3,070 kcal instead of the internally consistent 3,036 kcal); all nutrition values reported here use the single, current coefficient set; (ii) the legacy emission-factor tables did not define the ageing scenarios (A6/B6/C6), leaving their emissions unreported; the footprint module below applies scenario-invariant coefficients and therefore covers all 19 scenarios.

### CASM-World and the transmission experiment

CASM-World is used as a sequentially linked, one-way transmission model rather than a fully coupled equilibrium with CASM. It is a 13-region, 31-commodity world agricultural market model in the tradition of the ERS/Penn State (PEATSim-type) partial-equilibrium framework³⁰, calibrated to a 2023 base year and solved annually to 2050 with world market-clearing residuals ≤ 7 × 10⁻⁹. Regions are the United States, EU-15, Brazil, Argentina, Australia, New Zealand, Canada, Japan, South Korea, India, Mexico, China and an aggregate rest-of-world; commodities span the staple grains, oilseeds and their oil/meal products, sugar, and the pork, beef, poultry and dairy complexes (butter, cheese, whole and skim milk powder, fluid-equivalent raw milk). China's dietary scenarios enter as annual food-demand preference shifters from 2025 onward, superimposed on CASM-World's income-driven baseline growth; each region's supply, demand and net trade then adjust through world prices. CASM-World results are therefore the equilibrium response to an externally imposed China demand-preference path, not a model in which world prices are iterated back into domestic CASM consumption. Sheep meat, eggs, aquatic products, fruit and vegetables lie outside the world model's commodity space, so the global transmission of the HDS aquatic-product expansion is not captured; the sign of this omitted-channel bias is not known without an aquaculture-feed sensitivity. The commodity mapping, shifter construction and solver diagnostics are documented in the repository.

### Scenario design

Nineteen scenarios combine three dietary pathways plus a baseline with six demographic variants (Table 1). The **baseline (BS)** holds income elasticities of food demand fixed at calibrated 2023 levels, so per-capita demand evolves only with income and demography. The **preference-driven transition (PTS; scenarios A1–A6)** lets income elasticities evolve with per-capita GDP by linear interpolation across the income tiers estimated from cross-country demand regularities²⁶,²⁷, capturing staple saturation and continued animal-food upgrading. The **healthy-diet scenario (HDS; B1–B6)** converges per-capita consumption of each food group from its 2023 value to the composite healthy-diet benchmark (below) by 2050 along a constant-growth-rate path, q(t) = q(2023)·(1+r)^(t−2023) with r = (target/q(2023))^(1/27) − 1. The **moderate transition (MTS; C1–C6)** sets each commodity's annual per-capita demand growth to the arithmetic mean of the corresponding PTS and HDS growth rates, i.e. a 50% transition depth. Within each pathway, variant 1 is medium population with medium urbanisation; variants 2/3 are the UN World Population Prospects 2024 high (1.420 billion in 2035; 1.389 billion in 2050) and low (1.345 billion; 1.200 billion) population variants³¹; variants 4/5 are high and low urbanisation paths around the medium anchors of 75.4% (2035) and 81.4% (2050); and variant 6 is the ageing adjustment, which (i) converts head-count population into adult-equivalent consumers using age–sex dietary-energy requirement weights (1.4097 billion equivalents in 2023, 1.3970 billion in 2035, 1.2697 billion in 2050) and (ii) adds an ageing term to the income elasticities (−0.0314 per percentage-point rise in the old-age share). The medium population projection—1.4097 billion (2023), 1.3673 billion (2035), 1.2586 billion (2050)—is from the Institute of Quantitative and Technical Economics, Chinese Academy of Social Sciences; high/low variants are UN WPP 2024³¹. Aggregate demand is Q(s,c,t) = q(pathway,c,t) × POP(variant,t), with meat totals decomposed into pork, beef, mutton and poultry using pathway-specific internal shares. The full 19-scenario matrix, per-capita paths, elasticity tables and macro assumptions are released as machine-readable files (Supplementary Table 1; Code availability).

### The composite healthy-diet benchmark

The 2050 healthy-diet target is constructed from five dietary reference systems: the Dietary Guidelines for Chinese Residents (2022)³², the EAT-Lancet planetary-health reference diet¹⁴, Taiwan's food-based dietary guidelines, Japan's official dietary guidance, and the Mediterranean diet as operationalised in public-health guidance. For each of 11 food groups, each system's recommended intake range (g per person per day) is harmonised to common food-group definitions; the composite band is the cross-system minimum and maximum of the systems' midpoints, and the composite target is the midpoint of that band (a Korean guideline was excluded because it specifies servings without gram weights). Recommended *intake* is converted to *purchase* quantities by dividing by food-group edible shares and scaling to kg per year, giving, for example, composite targets of 106.9 kg cereals, 109.5 kg dairy, 37.8 kg meat and 75.0 kg aquatic products per person per year; the full benchmark table, edible shares and implied annual growth rates from the 2023 CASM base are given in Supplementary Table 2. HDS converges to the composite target; the composite band appears in Fig. 1 as the benchmark envelope.

### Nutrition accounting

Food quantities are converted to nutrients as N(s,t) = Σ_g Q_g(s,t) × e_g × n_g, where e_g is the edible share and n_g the energy/protein/fat/carbohydrate content per 100 g of food group g, taken from the China Food Composition Tables³³ mapped to the 21 CASM food commodities (coefficient file in the repository). Macronutrient energy shares use 4/9/4 kcal g⁻¹ for protein/fat/carbohydrate. Guideline-comparison indicators (red-meat grams per day, fat-energy share, etc.) are computed on edible-intake basis.

### Environmental footprint accounting

Four footprints—GHG, blue water, reactive nitrogen and land—are computed from an internationally sourced coefficient library in which every value is transcribed from a cited primary source and mapped to CASM and CASM-World commodity codes; unsourced cells are left empty and flagged, never imputed. **Carbon (main accounting):** FAOSTAT emission-intensities (farm-gate/on-farm boundary; reference year 2023)³⁴, China-specific where published and world values elsewhere. These validate the model's legacy China livestock factors item-by-item (for example pig meat 0.96 versus 0.92 t CO₂e t⁻¹; cattle meat 15.19 versus 15.82), giving the previously uncited domestic factors a traceable international anchor. **Carbon (sensitivity S1):** Poore & Nemecek cradle-to-retail life-cycle coefficients¹², which include feed, land-use change, processing, transport, packaging and retail and are roughly an order of magnitude larger for monogastrics; farm-gate and life-cycle boundaries are reported separately and never blended (Extended Data Fig. 2). **Blue water:** Mekonnen & Hoekstra crop and farm-animal water footprints (China columns where available)³⁵,³⁶, with green, blue and grey components kept separate and only blue reported in headline results. **Reactive nitrogen:** crop N-fertiliser application rates by crop and country³⁷ combined with IPCC 2019 livestock N-excretion defaults³⁸ and food-chain virtual-N/supply-chain factors³⁹,⁴⁰. **Land:** Poore & Nemecek land occupation (m²·yr kg⁻¹, arable plus pasture)¹² for diet land occupation, alongside the model's own physical harvested cropland—the conservative, model-internal metric behind the 21.7 Mha figure. Coefficients are held at 2023 values across scenarios and years to isolate the diet-composition and trade signal; the decomposition against scenario-specific technology assumptions is reported in Results. China footprints are computed on both production and consumption (production + imports − exports) boundaries; consumption-boundary results are headline.

### The global net-effect (trade–emissions consistency) framework

To answer whether China's dietary transition reduces global environmental pressure or merely displaces it, we compute for every scenario s and indicator k:

ΔGlobal_k(s) = Σ_r Σ_c f_{k,r,c} × [X_{r,c}(s) − X_{r,c}(BS)],

where X_{r,c} is CASM-World equilibrium production of commodity c in region r in 2050 and f_{k,r,c} the farm-gate footprint coefficient. The global net effect therefore equals China's domestic change *plus* the trade-induced production adjustment of all partners, evaluated under a single coefficient boundary—eliminating the inconsistency of applying domestic emission factors to a trading economy. Splitting the sum into China and ex-China terms yields the benefit-incidence shares (77–96% ex-China under HDS) in Fig. 4. Because region-specific coefficients are unavailable for some commodity–region pairs, world-mean coefficients are used there; this affects levels more than scenario differences, which are driven by quantity changes.

### Validation and reproducibility

The Python CASM reproduces the original GAMS results to machine precision across all 19 scenarios and all reported variables (median relative deviation ≤ 1.4 × 10⁻¹⁶; worst manuscript-table deviation 1.8 × 10⁻³, attributable to 2-decimal rounding in the manuscript tables), and CASM-World converges with market-clearing residuals ≤ 7 × 10⁻⁹ in every year of every scenario (Extended Data Fig. 4). All input workbooks, scenario files, coefficient libraries with citations, model code, solved results (long-format CSV) and the validation report are openly available (Code availability), permitting cell-level replication of every number in this paper.
